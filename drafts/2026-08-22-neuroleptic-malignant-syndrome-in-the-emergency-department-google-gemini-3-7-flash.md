**Subject:** neuroleptic malignant syndrome in the emergency department

**Question.** How is neuroleptic malignant syndrome identified, differentiated, and managed in emergency department settings?

**Answer.** Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is recognized as a rare, potentially life-threatening idiosyncratic reaction primarily caused by dopamine receptor antagonists or dopamine agonist withdrawal. Emergency department presentations typically feature hyperthermia, generalized muscle rigidity, altered mental status, and autonomic instability, though atypical presentations may lack fever or severe rigidity. Routine laboratory findings often demonstrate elevated creatine kinase, leukocytosis, and metabolic disturbances. The available evidence appears limited to retrospective observational cohorts, systematic reviews of case reports, and narrative clinical series. Syntheses suggest that management relies on rapid cessation of causative agents, aggressive supportive care, active cooling, and adjunctive pharmacotherapy including benzodiazepines, dantrolene, or bromocriptine. Increased mortality appears associated with older age, marked autonomic instability, and elevated blood urea nitrogen and creatine kinase.

## What the evidence shows

- A systematic review of 15 case reports (6 atypical and 9 typical presentations) observed that lorazepam administration was associated with clinical improvement in 5 of 6 atypical cases and 7 of 9 typical cases, with 4 atypical cases resolving acute symptoms within 72 hours.[1]
- A clinical review of emergency management indicated that neuroleptic malignant syndrome stems from central dopaminergic blockade and may progress to severe complications such as rhabdomyolysis and renal failure.[2]
- A retrospective cross-sectional study of 18 emergency department patients diagnosed with neuroleptic malignant syndrome identified atypical antipsychotics as the most frequent causative agents, with non-survivors exhibiting statistically significant differences in blood pressure, blood urea nitrogen, creatine kinase, and mean platelet volume compared to survivors (p ≤ 0.05).[3]
- A neurological practice review noted that clinical manifestations are often non-specific and emphasized the diagnostic necessity of excluding clinical mimics such as meningitis, serotonin syndrome, and malignant catatonia.[4]
- A case report of an adolescent presenting with severe hyperthermia measuring 41.9°C illustrated diagnostic overlap between exertional heat stroke and neuroleptic malignant syndrome, which responded to multimodal management including active cooling, hydration, dantrolene, and bromocriptine.[5]
- A nursing practice report highlighted that increasing volumes of psychiatric emergencies in acute settings necessitate heightened clinical vigilance for dopamine antagonist adverse events.[6]

## What remains open

- The comparative efficacy of specific pharmacotherapies, such as bromocriptine and dantrolene versus supportive care and benzodiazepines alone, has not been evaluated in randomized controlled trials.
- Validated diagnostic criteria specifically tailored for atypical neuroleptic malignant syndrome presentations in emergency settings remain unavailable, potentially contributing to diagnostic delay.
- Optimal timing and agent selection for antipsychotic rechallenge following emergency stabilization lack prospective evidence and remain largely empirical.

## Glossary

- **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome** — A rare, life-threatening reaction to dopamine receptor antagonists characterized by hyperthermia, muscle rigidity, altered mental status, and autonomic instability.
- **Creatine kinase** — An enzyme found predominantly in skeletal muscle that is released into the blood during muscle breakdown.
- **Dysautonomia** — A malfunction of the autonomic nervous system causing fluctuations in blood pressure, heart rate, and temperature regulation.
