QuestionWhat health risks are associated with night shift work in working adults, and how strong is the evidence across sleep, cardiometabolic, cancer and mental health outcomes?

AnswerNight shift work appears consistently associated with disturbed sleep and with modest increases in cardiometabolic disease, though the size and causal status of these associations seem to vary sharply by outcome. Meta-analyses and large cohorts report higher odds of sleep disturbance, roughly a fifth to a quarter higher risk of cardiovascular disease and atrial fibrillation, higher risk of type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome, and more depressive symptoms, apparently more so in women. Cancer evidence is the weakest and openly contested: early nurse cohorts found excess breast and colorectal cancer with long exposure, whereas an updated meta-analysis of 57 studies found no overall association. Because nearly all of this evidence is observational, and exposure is usually self-reported, residual confounding by socioeconomic and lifestyle factors probably remains unresolved. No randomised trial has tested whether altering schedules changes disease incidence, so the estimates above are best read as associations rather than causal effects.

Keywords night shift work; circadian misalignment; shift work sleep disturbance; cardiovascular disease risk; type 2 diabetes; breast cancer; occupational health

What the evidence shows

Every figure in this review was located in the material read for the cited sources — their abstracts, and the open-access full text where one was retrieved.

  • A meta-analysis of 22 cross-sectional studies (21,677 participants, eight countries) reported shift work associated with higher odds of sleep disturbance (OR 1.42, 95% CI 1.24–1.62), with rotating schedules (OR 1.48) and healthcare professionals (OR 1.56) showing the largest estimates; the cross-sectional design leaves the direction of the association uncertain.1
  • In 131,844 UK Biobank workers followed a median of 13.78 years, usual or permanent night shifts were associated with incident cardiovascular disease (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.18–1.31), all-cause mortality (HR 1.23), atrial fibrillation (HR 1.31) and myocardial infarction (HR 1.29). Life's Essential 8 lifestyle score was estimated to mediate about 39% of the cardiovascular association, a figure that depends on the modelling assumptions used.2
  • A separate large UK Biobank analysis reported a graded increase in atrial fibrillation risk across night shift categories (usual/permanent nights HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.02–1.32) and higher coronary heart disease risk with ≥10 years of night work (HR 1.37), which appeared unmodified by genetic predisposition.3
  • Among 143,410 female nurses followed 22–24 years, each five-year increment of rotating night shift work carried a type 2 diabetes hazard ratio of 1.31 (95% CI 1.19–1.44), with a significant additive interaction with unhealthy lifestyle.4 UK Biobank cross-sectional data suggested higher diabetes odds with more night shifts per month (>8/month OR 1.36), though temporal ordering cannot be established from that design.5
  • Cancer findings conflict. In the Nurses' Health Study, ≥30 years of rotating nights was associated with breast cancer risk (RR 1.36, 95% CI 1.04–1.78) and ≥15 years with colorectal cancer risk (RR 1.35, 95% CI 1.03–1.77).6,7 By contrast, an updated meta-analysis of 57 studies pooling several million participants found no association of ever night shift work with breast, prostate, ovarian, colorectal or stomach cancer.8
  • A meta-analysis restricted to healthcare workers (12 studies, 12,132 breast cancer cases) found no excess for ever exposure (RR 1.05) and a modest excess at ≥20 years (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.01–1.55).9 That longer-duration estimate lost significance in influence analysis and approached unity after correction for publication bias, and 60% of studies were rated high risk of bias for exposure assessment, so it may be fragile.9
  • A meta-analysis of seven longitudinal studies reported shift work associated with adverse mental health outcomes (effect size 1.28, 95% CI 1.02–1.62) and depressive symptoms (1.33), with female shift workers apparently at higher odds of depressive symptoms (OR 1.73, 95% CI 1.39–2.14).10
  • A consensus discussion paper by 15 shift work researchers judged the evidence sufficient to propose schedule limits — no more than three consecutive night shifts, shift intervals of at least 11 hours, and night shifts no longer than nine hours — while conceding that the underlying literature is limited.11

What remains open

  • Whether night shift work causes cancer remains unresolved: long-duration nurse cohorts and the pooled healthcare-worker analysis point weakly upward, while the largest updated meta-analysis reports null associations and no apparent dose-response with cumulative exposure.6,8,9
  • No randomised or controlled intervention appears to have tested whether changing shift schedules alters incidence of cardiovascular disease, diabetes or cancer; scheduling recommendations seem to rest largely on short-term physiological and observational evidence.11
  • Exposure measurement is a persistent weakness — most cohorts rely on self-reported or baseline-only night work histories, and payroll-based data in a Danish healthcare cohort with low night work exposure showed no consistent exposure-response relation for coronary heart disease, though that cohort's limited exposure range may explain the null result.9,12
  • How much of the excess risk might be modifiable through lifestyle rather than intrinsic to circadian misalignment has been estimated only by mediation and counterfactual modelling within a single cohort, and does not appear to have been tested prospectively.2

Glossary

Circadian misalignment
A mismatch between the body's internal 24-hour clock and the timing of sleep, light exposure and meals imposed by the work schedule.
Hazard ratio
The ratio of the rate at which an event occurs in an exposed group compared with an unexposed group over follow-up time.
Mediation analysis
A statistical method estimating how much of an exposure's effect on an outcome operates through an intermediate factor, such as lifestyle.
Life's Essential 8
An American Heart Association composite score of cardiovascular health covering diet, activity, nicotine, sleep, weight, blood pressure, lipids and glucose.
Publication bias
The tendency for studies with positive or striking results to be published more often, which can inflate pooled estimates in a meta-analysis.
