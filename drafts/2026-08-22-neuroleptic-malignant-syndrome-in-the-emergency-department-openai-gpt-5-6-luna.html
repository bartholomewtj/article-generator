QuestionWhat is known about neuroleptic malignant syndrome in the emergency department?

AnswerNeuroleptic malignant syndrome in the emergency department is supported mainly by retrospective cohorts and case reports rather than comparative trials. The syndrome may present with the familiar combination of hyperthermia, rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability, and muscle injury, but atypical cases can lack fever, marked rigidity, or substantial creatine kinase elevation. Diagnostic uncertainty is substantial because malignant catatonia, serotonin toxicity, infection, heat illness, and metabolic disease can overlap clinically. Complications described in emergency presentations include rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, respiratory failure, metabolic derangement, recurrent episodes, and death. A systematic review of case reports found improvement after lorazepam in many reported cases, but treatment effects remain highly uncertain because the evidence is uncontrolled and heterogeneous.

What the evidence shows

Every figure in this review was located in the material read for the cited sources — their abstracts, and the open-access full text where one was retrieved.

  • A retrospective emergency-department study of 18 patients with neuroleptic malignant syndrome found a mean age of 46.9 years, equal representation of women and men, and cases associated with antipsychotics, levodopa withdrawal, or metoclopramide; blood pressure, blood urea nitrogen, creatine kinase, and mean platelet volume differed between survivors and nonsurvivors.1
  • Chen and Bae’s systematic review of case reports and case series found clinical improvement after lorazepam in 5 of 6 atypical cases and 7 of 9 typical cases; the evidence was limited to uncontrolled reports.2
  • A case report of a 23-year-old man presenting with diabetic ketoacidosis found concurrent clozapine-associated neuroleptic malignant syndrome despite absent rigidity and only a modest creatine kinase increase; fever, altered consciousness, and acidosis improved after clozapine discontinuation.3
  • Elyasi and colleagues’ case report described a man with schizophrenia who experienced three episodes associated with risperidone; the current emergency presentation included decreased consciousness, fever, rigidity, and creatine phosphokinase of 14,949 U/L.4
  • A case report of a man transferred to the emergency department after prolonged psychiatric hospitalization found severe rigidity, stupor, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, metabolic acidosis, and acute kidney injury, followed by partial recovery but persistent dystonia and functional impairment.5
  • A case report of a 25-year-old man with schizophrenia found persistent hyperthermia, autonomic symptoms, and worsening mental status despite creatine phosphokinase values of 669 and 710 U/L; the patient died after nine days of hospitalization.6

What remains open

  • No prospective emergency-department study has validated diagnostic criteria for atypical neuroleptic malignant syndrome against important mimics such as malignant catatonia, serotonin toxicity, infection, or heat illness.
  • Comparative trials have not established whether lorazepam, bromocriptine, dantrolene, amantadine, or supportive care produces better outcomes in emergency presentations.
  • The extent to which creatine kinase concentration, blood pressure, renal indices, or other laboratory markers independently predict mortality remains uncertain beyond small retrospective cohorts.1,2

Glossary

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome
A rare, potentially fatal reaction associated mainly with dopamine-blocking or other dopamine-modulating medicines.
Atypical neuroleptic malignant syndrome
A presentation in which one or more usual features, such as fever, severe rigidity, or marked creatine kinase elevation, are absent or mild.
Rhabdomyolysis
Breakdown of skeletal muscle that releases intracellular substances into the circulation and can cause kidney injury.
Malignant catatonia
A severe catatonic state that can include stupor, rigidity, fever, and autonomic instability and may resemble neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
