Interventions for night shift workers show modest but real benefits, concentrated in sleep outcomes

AbstractNight shift work exposes a substantial fraction of the global workforce to circadian misalignment, curtailed sleep, and downstream risks to metabolic, cardiovascular, and mental health. Because rotating and permanent night schedules are unlikely to disappear from health care, transport, manufacturing, and other essential sectors, there is a long-standing interest in interventions that might blunt these harms without requiring workers to leave shift work altogether. Approaches under study range from pharmacological and behavioral sleep treatments to light exposure, physical activity, nutrition timing, napping, and organizational scheduling changes, but the evidence supporting each has developed unevenly. Here the evidence shows that sleep-directed interventions, particularly cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia and structured napping, have the most consistent support, with a recent meta-analysis reporting a moderate pooled effect across intervention types, while interventions targeting metabolic and cardiovascular endpoints remain largely exploratory or still in trial. Organizational-level changes to working-time arrangements carry stronger systematic-review support than most individual-level programs, though evidence for many outcomes stays inconclusive. This review considers the mechanistic rationale, the intervention categories tested to date, and the gaps that limit translation of laboratory and small-trial findings into durable occupational health policy for shift-based industries.

Keywords night shift work; circadian misalignment; shift work sleep disorder; cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia; occupational health intervention; napping; light therapy; rotating shift schedule

Key points

  • A meta-analysis pooling 25 randomized and quasi-experimental trials in rotating night shift workers reported a moderate overall effect (Hedges' g = 0.59, 95% CI 0.33-0.84) across pharmacological, light therapy, cognitive behavioral, aroma/alternative, and schedule-modification interventions.1
  • A four-week online cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia program improved sleep efficiency by about 7.2%, statistically indistinguishable from a 7.7% gain in a parallel face-to-face outpatient sample, with significant improvements also seen in well-being and insomnia severity scores.2
  • A review of five dietary intervention studies covering 670 night shift workers found no weight loss at six or twelve months but did report reduced LDL-cholesterol at six months, alongside shifts in postprandial triglyceride, glucose, and insulin peaks depending on meal timing.7
  • A systematic review of 14 high-quality studies found shift work including nights raises accident risk by roughly 50% to 100%, but suggested that continuous, non-rotating night work may partly protect against this risk through circadian resynchronization, unlike rotating schedules.12
  • Controlled crossover laboratory studies showed that circadian misalignment alone, independent of sleep duration, significantly worsened mood and well-being over four days in both non-shift and shift worker participants, supporting a circadian rather than purely sleep-loss mechanism for mood effects.6
  • An ongoing registered randomized trial (NAPWORK) is testing whether a 30-minute nap opportunity during 12-hour night shifts reduces endothelial dysfunction and other cardiovascular biomarkers in 80 nurses, illustrating that cardiovascular-outcome evidence for napping is still being generated rather than established.8

Introduction

Work scheduled outside conventional daytime hours affects a substantial share of the workforce in industrialized economies, with estimates suggesting roughly one in six or one in five employees works some form of shift schedule. Night shift work forces a chronic mismatch between the endogenous circadian pacemaker and the timing of sleep, activity, and food intake, and this misalignment has been implicated in impaired sleep, cognitive performance, mood, and long-term disease risk. Because rotating and fixed night schedules remain operationally necessary in health care, transport, manufacturing, and emergency services, reducing schedules to daytime-only work is rarely a feasible solution.

This review considers what has actually been tested to improve health and well-being in this population, rather than only what mechanisms have been proposed. The evidence base is uneven: some domains, particularly sleep-directed behavioral and pharmacological treatment, have been evaluated in multiple randomized trials and at least one meta-analysis, while others, including nutrition timing, napping for cardiovascular risk, and organizational scheduling reform, rest on a smaller number of trials, protocols still underway, or narrative syntheses. The sections that follow group the evidence by outcome domain and intervention type, noting where the evidence is direct, where it is extrapolated from related conditions, and where trials are still in progress.

Box 1 | Key study: Effectiveness of sleep interventions for rotating night shift workers: a systematic review and meta-analysis

Jeon et al. (2023) · Frontiers in Public Health1 · doi:10.3389/fpubh.2023.1187382

It is the only systematic review and meta-analysis in this set that quantitatively pools outcomes across the full range of sleep intervention categories tested in rotating night shift workers, giving the most direct summary estimate of intervention effectiveness.

Method. The authors searched six databases for randomized controlled and quasi-experimental trials published between January 1990 and June 2022, appraised quality with the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist, and pooled 25 of 30 eligible studies in a random-effects meta-analysis covering pharmacological, light therapy, cognitive behavioral, aroma/alternative, and shift-schedule-modification approaches.

Results. The overall pooled effect size across all intervention categories was moderate (Hedges' g = 0.59, 95% confidence interval 0.33-0.84, p < 0.001), indicating that sleep interventions as a group meaningfully reduced sleep disturbance or promoted sleep among rotating night shift workers.

Sleep-directed behavioral and pharmacological interventions

Sleep disturbance is the most extensively studied target for intervention in night shift populations, and it is also the domain with the clearest synthesis of trial evidence. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 25 randomized or quasi-experimental trials in rotating night shift workers reported a moderate pooled effect across pharmacological, light therapy, cognitive behavioral, aroma or alternative therapy, and shift-schedule-modification approaches, with an overall Hedges' g of 0.59.1 This synthesis indicates that sleep interventions as a class are effective in this population, though the moderate pooled size and the diversity of approaches folded into a single estimate mean that no single technique can be assumed to work equally well for every worker or setting.

Among behavioral approaches, a comparison of online and face-to-face cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia in shift workers found that a four-week online program produced sleep efficiency gains of about 7.2%, closely matching the 7.7% improvement seen in a face-to-face outpatient sample, with no significant difference in the rate of improvement between delivery formats.2 In the online arm, well-being and insomnia severity scores also improved significantly following treatment, while the outpatient sample showed parallel improvement in depression and sleep-quality measures, suggesting that remotely delivered behavioral treatment may be a feasible substitute where in-person services are unavailable.2 A mini-review focused on night shift nurses similarly concluded that sleep problems are near-universal in this occupation and argued for individualized interventions tailored to psychosocial and environmental factors that mediate a given worker's tolerance of night shifts, rather than a single standardized protocol, although this recommendation is based on synthesis of prior literature rather than a new controlled trial.3

Pharmacological options, including melatonin and hypnotics, are frequently used to manage shift work sleep disorder, but a review focused on shift work and men's health concluded that such treatments, alongside planned naps and timed light exposure, are limited by an absence of strong efficacy evidence, and it called explicitly for further controlled research before firm treatment guidelines could be established.4 This caution tempers the more favorable pooled estimate reported elsewhere, since the meta-analysis result reflects heterogeneous designs and comparators rather than confirming any one pharmacological regimen.1

Circadian alignment and activity-based countermeasures

A biological rationale for many proposed interventions rests on the observation that circadian misalignment itself, independent of sleep loss, appears to impair mood and well-being. Randomized within-subject crossover laboratory studies in both non-shift and shift workers found that simulated night shifts inducing a mismatch between the endogenous pacemaker and the sleep-wake cycle significantly reduced mood and well-being over four days of continuous exposure, compared with circadian-aligned conditions, in both groups.6 Because this was a controlled physiological study rather than a workplace intervention trial, it does not itself test a remedy, but it strengthens the mechanistic case for interventions that specifically target circadian phase rather than sleep duration alone.

Physical activity has been proposed as one such circadian-adaptation strategy, on the reasoning that it is accessible and comparatively low-cost relative to controlled light exposure, which is difficult to implement outside occupations where lighting can be tightly managed. A conceptual review proposed that physical activity could influence adaptation across three timeframes—within a single shift, across a block of shifts, and across a working career—and that its effects might extend to markers beyond circadian phase, including cognition and subjective well-being.5 This remains a theoretical framework rather than a tested intervention, and the authors themselves note that non-photic adaptation strategies, including exercise, are comparatively under-researched relative to light-based circadian countermeasures.5 Light therapy is included as one of several intervention categories in the broader sleep meta-analysis, but its effects were pooled together with other approaches rather than reported separately, limiting what can be said about its effect size in isolation.1

Metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes

Night shift work is consistently associated with elevated cardiometabolic risk, but trials testing interventions against biological endpoints in this domain are fewer and, in some cases, still underway. A literature review of dietary interventions identified only five eligible studies covering 670 night shift workers across healthcare, industrial, and public safety settings; longer nutrition programs did not achieve weight loss at six or twelve months, but low-density lipoprotein cholesterol improved at six months, and shorter meal-timing adjustments shifted the timing of triglyceride, glucose, and insulin peaks depending on when meals were consumed.7 The review's authors explicitly called for randomized trials examining energy reduction and meal timing together with hunger, sleepiness, and performance outcomes, indicating that this evidence base remains preliminary.7

A registered randomized controlled trial protocol, the NAPWORK study, is testing whether a 30-minute nap opportunity during 12-hour night shifts, compared with an equivalent rest period without sleep, can reduce early cardiovascular risk biomarkers such as endothelial dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and heart rate variability in 80 nurses and assistant nurses.8 Because this is a protocol description rather than a completed trial, no outcome data are yet available, but the design illustrates a shift toward testing mechanistic cardiovascular endpoints rather than relying solely on self-reported fatigue or sleepiness.8 A separate narrative review on night shift work and type 2 diabetes proposed that interventions improving sleep quality, realigning circadian rhythms, and normalizing melatonin secretion could plausibly reduce diabetes risk, but this synthesis draws on mechanistic and observational literature rather than intervention trials, and it explicitly frames further mechanistic study as a prerequisite for effective countermeasures.9 A review of night shift work and the gut microbiome extends this mechanistic reasoning further, proposing that circadian-aligned nutrition, time-restricted eating, and probiotic or prebiotic supplementation might restore microbiome-host synchrony disrupted by night work, though this too is an extrapolation from broader chronobiology and microbiome literature rather than evidence from trials conducted specifically in shift worker cohorts.10

Scheduling and organizational-level approaches

Interventions applied at the level of the schedule or the organization, rather than the individual worker, have somewhat stronger systematic-review support for certain outcomes than most individual behavioral or pharmacological programs. An overview of 52 systematic reviews covering organizational-level workplace interventions found strong-quality evidence specifically for interventions that changed working-time arrangements, alongside moderate-quality evidence for changes to work tasks, organization, or the psychosocial work environment more broadly.11 That overview also found strong evidence that organizational interventions reduced burnout and moderate evidence of benefit across various other health and well-being outcomes, though evidence for many other outcome-intervention combinations remained low-quality or inconclusive, and the authors called for more research on implementation and context.11

Scheduling matters not only for health but for safety. A systematic review of 14 high-quality studies on shift and long working hours found that shift work including night shifts carries a substantially increased risk of workplace accidents, with observed increases typically ranging from 50% to 100%, and that risk of accidents rises further as shift length extends beyond eight hours, roughly doubling by around twelve hours.12 Notably, the same review reported that

pure night work, worked consistently rather than rotated, may confer some protection against accident risk through circadian resynchronization, a finding that complicates any blanket recommendation to minimize night shifts, since rotating schedules may carry different risks than fixed ones.12 This distinction between rotating and fixed night schedules recurs as a source of disagreement across the literature and suggests that intervention design should specify which schedule type is being targeted rather than treating night shift work as a uniform exposure.

Conclusions and outlook

Across the sources reviewed, the strongest and most direct evidence supports sleep-directed behavioral interventions, with a meta-analysis of 25 trials reporting a moderate overall benefit across pharmacological, light-based, cognitive behavioral, and schedule-modification approaches,1 and individual trials showing that online-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia can match face-to-face treatment in improving sleep efficiency and well-being.2 Organizational-level changes to working-time arrangements also carry comparatively strong systematic-review support for certain outcomes, including burnout,11 and accident-risk data underline that schedule structure, particularly shift length and rotation versus fixed night work, materially affects safety outcomes.12

Evidence for interventions targeting metabolic, cardiovascular, and gut-microbiome pathways is considerably less mature. Dietary intervention trials remain few and have not demonstrated weight loss, though lipid improvements have been reported;7 a trial testing napping against cardiovascular biomarkers is still in progress;8 and proposals involving microbiome-targeted nutrition,10 physical activity as a non-photic circadian countermeasure,5 and diabetes-prevention strategies built on circadian and melatonin mechanisms9 remain largely extrapolated from mechanistic or observational literature rather than confirmed in controlled trials among night shift workers specifically. Laboratory evidence that circadian misalignment itself impairs mood independent of sleep loss6 strengthens the rationale for circadian-targeted interventions but does not itself constitute an intervention test. Closing this gap will require randomized trials with cardiometabolic and mental-health endpoints, direct comparison of rotating versus fixed schedules, and attention to whether individual-level programs perform differently when combined with organizational scheduling reform rather than deployed alone.

Glossary

circadian misalignment
A mismatch between the body's internal biological clock and the actual timing of sleep, wakefulness, and eating.
cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)
A structured, non-drug talk-therapy program that changes sleep-related habits and thoughts to improve sleep.
shift work sleep disorder
A recognized sleep disorder in which shift work causes persistent insomnia or excessive sleepiness tied to the work schedule.
Hedges' g
A statistical measure of effect size used to compare results across different studies in a meta-analysis, similar in interpretation to a standardized mean difference.
endothelial dysfunction
Impaired function of the cells lining blood vessels, an early marker of cardiovascular disease risk.
dysbiosis
An imbalance in the normal community of microorganisms living in the gut.
