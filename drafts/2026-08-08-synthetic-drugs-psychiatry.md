# Novel psychoactive substances in acute psychiatry: synthetic cannabinoids and synthetic cathinones

*Generated 8 August 2026 · 17 sources cited, 2014–2026 · Full text read for 5 of 17 sources · Not peer reviewed*

**Subject:** Novel psychoactive substances and acute psychiatric harm: synthetic cannabinoids and cathinones

**Abstract.** Novel psychoactive substances are synthetic compounds engineered to reproduce the effects of controlled drugs while falling outside the legislation that governs them. More than a thousand are now known, and two classes account for most of the psychiatric burden attributed to the group: synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists and synthetic cathinones. Both arrive in emergency and psychiatric services as agitation, paranoia and frank psychosis, and neither is reliably detected by the antibody-based urine screens that most services run, so the exposure is often invisible at the point of assessment. Here we review the evidence on acute and persisting psychiatric harm from these two classes, drawing on systematic reviews, hospital case series and toxicologically confirmed intoxication cohorts. The evidence indicates that receptor agonists carry a more severe and longer psychotic course than cannabis. Cathinones appear to produce a stimulant picture close to methamphetamine while diverging sharply in physical complication, and the two classes differ in how long their symptoms and their analytes persist. Most of the literature remains retrospective, and prospective psychiatric follow-up after analytically confirmed exposure is scarce, so the incidence of persisting illness is not established. Analytical sensitivity and surveillance capacity, rather than unfamiliar pharmacology, appear to be the limiting factors.

**Keywords:** novel psychoactive substances; synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists; synthetic cathinones; substance-induced psychosis; toxicovigilance; emergency psychiatry

## Introduction

Illicit drug markets have carried a parallel synthetic catalogue since the mid-2000s. These compounds are built from the molecular scaffolds of controlled drugs, sold through internet vendors as products labelled not for human consumption, and reformulated as soon as a jurisdiction schedules them.[1,2] More than 1200 are now known; 566 were reported to the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime during 2022, and roughly 400 were circulating in the European market at the end of 2023.[5] A clinician assessing an intoxicated patient therefore works without the pharmacological reference points that accumulate around an established drug.

Two classes dominate the psychiatric burden. Synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists bind CB1 with greater potency and full efficacy, where tetrahydrocannabinol is only a partial agonist, while synthetic cathinones act on monoamine transporters in the manner of amphetamine-type stimulants.[3] Of the 163 substances reported to European monitoring between 2010 and 2012, 39.3% were synthetic cannabinoids and 16.6% synthetic cathinones.[4] Their share of clinical presentations exceeds their share of the catalogue.

Here we review what the published work establishes about the mental health consequences of using these two drug groups, and what it leaves open. The scope is clinical rather than chemical: presentation, course, detection and outcome, rather than the question of which analogue emerges next. Sources range from systematic reviews of a whole class to single-centre series of analytically confirmed intoxications, and their evidential weight differs accordingly. Where these lines of work disagree, the discrepancy is likely to reflect that difference in design rather than a genuine change in the drugs.

## Acute presentations of synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists

Use is uncommon in the general population but concentrated in the young. Lifetime use of these agonists runs between 0.2 and 4% in national surveys, against roughly a quarter to a third for cannabis; among United States college students the figure reaches 6 to 17%, and approximately 1% of Europeans aged 14 to 18 report having used them at least once.[6] Clinical and preclinical work links adolescent cannabinoid exposure to raised later risk of schizophrenia, which is what makes that age distribution matter rather than merely describe it.

The acute syndrome itself is documented in some detail. A systematic review registered with PROSPERO assembled 109 intoxication cases published between 2013 and 2024 in which psychiatric symptoms were reported; agonist-related cases formed the largest group at 57, with a mean age of 24.4 years across a range from 14 to 59.[5] Hallucinations dominated, in 51 cases, followed by aggressiveness in 24, anxiety in 14, and paranoia and psychosis in 11 each. Chronic sequelae were recorded after almost 8% of those intoxications, though that figure is likely to depend on how long each report happened to follow its patient.

Whether exposure initiates a psychotic illness or unmasks a latent vulnerability remains unresolved. A systematic review that screened 4477 records and retained 95 identified these agonists as the substances most frequently linked to acute psychosis, and noted Schneiderian first-rank symptoms in some presentations, an overlap with schizophrenia that the authors read as a possible risk of transition.[7] Many episodes appeared transient, yet a proportion evolved into persistent psychotic states, an observation compatible with either causal account.

Description from the ward is thinner. Four patients admitted to acute psychiatric services with psychosis and confirmed misuse were assessed with the PANSS and monitored with the National Early Warning Signs tool; management turned on identifying the index compound, increasing nursing observation and tracking vital signs.[8] A series of four cannot establish a syndrome, and findings from so small a group might not generalise beyond the unit that produced them.

## Synthetic cathinones and the stimulant picture

Cathinones present as stimulant intoxication, and the closest available comparison is with methamphetamine. A psychiatric emergency department in Taiwan handling some 3350 visits a year screened patients presenting with acute delirium, first-episode psychosis or a known history of use, and identified 24 cathinone intoxications between April 2019 and May 2020, matching each by age and sex against two methamphetamine controls.[9] Methcathinone was the commonest compound at 66.7%, with eutylone and mephedrone at 25% each, a distribution that presumably tracks local supply rather than any property of the class.

Psychiatrically the two groups were hard to tell apart. Delusions occurred in 79.2% of the cathinone group against 66.7% of the methamphetamine group and hallucinations in 58.3% against 47.9%, with violent behaviour in over half of each; neither the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale nor the Clinical Global Impression scores differed significantly, the former standing at 71.7 against 67.3.[9] Suicide attempts were in fact somewhat less frequent among the cathinone patients, at 12.5% against 22.9%. The acute mental state alone is unlikely to distinguish the two exposures at the bedside.

The separation was physical. Complications occurred in 45.8% of cathinone patients against 8.3% of methamphetamine patients, an association that survived adjustment for symptom severity at an odds ratio of 8.55 with a confidence interval from 2.15 to 34.03.[9] Those patients also ran warmer, at 37.0 against 36.6 degrees, and had more substances detected concurrently. Their social profile inverted the usual expectation: none had a family history of substance use, against 18.8% of controls, and criminal histories were less common at 20.8% against 52.1%. Whether the physical excess reflects the compounds themselves or the polydrug pattern around them cannot be settled by a study of this size.

Class reviews place that picture in a wider frame. Chronic cathinone use can induce acute psychosis, hypomania, paranoid ideation and delusions resembling amphetamine-type stimulant psychosis,[10] and severe intoxication has been linked to serotonin syndrome and to potentially fatal rhabdomyolysis.[4] Among the 29 cathinone reports in the case-based review, subjects were older than users of other classes at a mean of 30.9 years, mephedrone featured in 28% and MDPV in 21%, and a third carried a personal or family psychiatric history.[5]

Time course separates the two classes in a way that matters at triage. A Japanese series of six patients aged 22 to 42 was analysed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Cathinone-related psychiatric and neurological symptoms proved long-lasting, with the compounds detectable in serum for 15 to 48 hours, whereas cannabinoid symptoms resolved within several hours and serum concentrations stayed at or below 5 ng/mL for one to three hours.[11] One patient with high concentrations of both drugs died, heart failure being judged the most plausible cause. Six patients are too few to fix these windows precisely, but the correspondence between analyte persistence and symptom duration would be expected on pharmacokinetic grounds.

## Receptor pharmacology and the potency problem

The clinical picture tracks the receptor pharmacology closely enough that the class largely predicts the toxidrome. Stimulant compounds either inhibit dopamine and noradrenaline transport or induce release of those monoamines; entactogens predominantly enhance serotonin release; hallucinogens act as direct agonists at 5-HT2A receptors; and synthetic cannabinoids agonise CB1.[1] The ratio of dopamine to serotonin transporter inhibition measured in vitro has been proposed as a predictor of both the sought effect and the toxicity. Serotonergic compounds tend to be associated with serotonin syndrome, hyperthermia, seizures and hyponatraemia, and dopaminergic compounds with prolonged stimulation, insomnia, agitation and psychosis.[1]

That ratio has since been given numbers. Compounds whose transporter ratio falls below 0.1 are likely to produce entactogenic, MDMA-like effects, while those above 10 produce distinctly psychostimulant effects and carry high abuse potential.[2] The same review placed the cannabinoid difference not in an unfamiliar target but in full agonism at CB1, and identified hyperthermia as a principal route to the severe complications of stimulant designer drugs, rhabdomyolysis and renal failure among them.

Potency, then, rather than novelty of mechanism. JWH-018, among the earliest compounds identified in these products, has four times the affinity of tetrahydrocannabinol for CB1 and ten times its affinity for CB2, and at least 200 distinct agonists have been isolated from herbal mixtures since 2008.[6] A pharmacological review counted 207 substances detected by European monitoring between 2008 and October 2020 and attributed the association with acute and chronic intoxication to that full agonism.[12] An update covering stimulant and hallucinogenic compounds grouped their effects into raised central dopamine, activation of 5-HT receptor subtypes and antagonism at NMDA receptors, and argued that clinicians require regular briefing on what is circulating.[13]

Review of individual compounds — mitragynine, the Spice cannabinoids, mephedrone and methylone, ketamine and gamma-hydroxybutyrate among them — shows the same pattern of a known target reached by an unfamiliar molecule.[3] Sex and gender differences remain largely uncharacterised for this group, although men present more often to emergency departments with serious or fatal intoxication, while women seem equally likely to develop a substance use disorder and possibly more susceptible to craving and relapse.[14]

## Detection and the limits of routine screening

Routine screening does not see these compounds, and that absence shapes every clinical encounter with them. Both classes often escape the panels used in screening tests.[4] The case-based systematic review treated missing toxicological confirmation as a source of diagnostic error rather than a technical inconvenience: across its 109 cases only 36 molecules were ever analytically confirmed, while 37 could name nothing more precise than a drug class.[5] A diagnosis of substance-induced psychosis otherwise rests on an exposure the laboratory has never documented, so the true frequency of these presentations is uncertain and published counts are probably underestimates.

The gap is one of sensitivity as much as of coverage. The Taiwanese department detected its cathinones by liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry, which covers more than 100 such substances down to 50 ng/mL of urine, against the 500 ng/mL threshold of the immunoassay panel running alongside it.[9] An order of magnitude in detection limit is what separates seeing the drug from not seeing it. How far that shortfall accounts for the low confirmation rates reported elsewhere remains unclear, since assay coverage and assay sensitivity fail in the same direction.

A retrospective multicentre series from Reunion Island quantifies what that costs in practice. Of 176 patients admitted to four public hospitals with suspected exposure between August 2022 and March 2024, 152 (86%) underwent biological analysis and 14 (9.2%) were confirmed positive by high-resolution mass spectrometry.[15] The confirmed compounds spanned synthetic cathinones, synthetic cannabinoids, a novel opioid and an aminoindane, and clinical severity ranged from agitation and sedation through to cardiovascular collapse, rhabdomyolysis and respiratory failure.

That cohort also describes who presents. Intentional use accounted for 77.3% of exposures and fell overwhelmingly to men, while unintentional exposure made up the remainder and was significantly more frequent among women.[15] Previous psychoactive substance use was reported by 92.9% of the confirmed group against 42.0% of the rest, and alcohol co-exposure was less common in confirmed cases than in conventional intoxications. On these figures a polydrug history is a better prompt to test than any single presenting sign.

## Course, mortality and surveillance

Evidence on what follows the acute episode is the weakest part of this literature, and the most recent systematic review is explicit about why. Searching six databases for January 2005 to August 2025 under PRISMA 2020, it retained 58 studies, of which 28 (48.3%) offered at least six months of psychiatric follow-up and 22 (37.9%) reported acute toxicity or fatalities.[16] The same proportion again was rated high quality. Estimates of persisting illness drawn from a base of this composition are consistent with a wide range of true values.

Where follow-up existed, the figures point one way. Hospitalisation for agonist-related psychosis ran markedly longer than for cannabis-related psychosis in one comparative cohort, at 43.45 against 22.91 days, and a case series recorded psychotic episodes persisting beyond five months in approximately 30% of patients.[16] Rhabdomyolysis reached 63% in a cathinone outbreak series, while NBOMe exposures carried a fatality rate near 15% and intensive care admission near 40%.

Mortality surveillance adds a policy dimension that clinical series cannot. Fifteen years of post-mortem toxicology reported to a national programme in England, Wales and Northern Ireland showed detections of novel stimulants peaking in 2015, novel opioids in 2017, and cannabinoid receptor agonists in 2018 and again in 2021.[17] The generic Chinese ban on cannabinoid structural families in 2021 was followed by their near-total disappearance from these deaths in 2022, whereas domestic controls introduced earlier had no comparable effect. Post-mortem detection is a lagging and selective measure, so the timing of these peaks may reflect testing practice as well as supply.

That pattern was anticipated at the level of the individual molecule. United Kingdom controls on first-generation agonists in 2009 and on a second generation in 2013 were each followed by structural modification rather than by disappearance, which is why legislation aimed at named substances has been argued to be insufficient on its own.[6] Whether the compounds that replaced them were more harmful, or merely newer, is not established by detection counts alone.

## Key points

- In a matched psychiatric emergency-department comparison, delusions and hallucinations were about as common in 24 cathinone intoxications as in 48 methamphetamine intoxications, but physical complications occurred in 45.8% against 8.3%.[9]
- Across 109 published intoxication cases carrying psychiatric symptoms, hallucinations were reported in 51 and aggressiveness in 24, and chronic sequelae followed almost 8% of the agonist-related cases.[5]
- Mass spectrometry detected synthetic cathinones down to 50 ng/mL of urine where the immunoassay running alongside it required 500 ng/mL, so the exposure is routinely missed rather than absent.[9]
- Hospitalisation for agonist-related psychosis lasted 43.45 days against 22.91 for cannabis-related psychosis in one comparative cohort, and roughly 30% of one case series remained psychotic beyond five months.[16]
- Serum detection windows diverge sharply between the classes: 15 to 48 hours for cathinones against one to three hours for cannabinoids in a six-patient series, matching the reported duration of symptoms.[11]
- Detections of cannabinoid receptor agonists in United Kingdom deaths fell almost to zero in 2022 after generic controls were introduced in China in 2021, while earlier domestic legislation showed no equivalent effect.[17]

## Conclusions and outlook

Two findings rest on more than a single study. Synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists produce psychosis that is more severe and longer-running than the cannabis-related form, supported by systematic review, comparative hospitalisation data and repeated case description.[5,7,16] Cathinones produce a psychotic and behavioural presentation close to methamphetamine while carrying greater physical risk, supported by a matched emergency-department comparison and by class reviews.[9,10]

What stays open is the direction of causation in psychosis, the true incidence of persisting illness, and whether outcome differs between analogues within a class. Nearly every source here is retrospective or a narrative synthesis; the prospective element, where there is one, is the toxicological confirmation rather than the psychiatric follow-up. A cohort that confirmed exposure analytically at presentation and then tracked psychiatric outcome for a year or more would settle more than any further case series.

The practical implication is unglamorous. Analytical capacity and toxicovigilance networks, not new therapeutics, are what these sources repeatedly identify as missing, given that treatment for the intoxications themselves is supportive.[1] The detection thresholds are the concrete form of that argument: an assay an order of magnitude more sensitive is the difference between a documented exposure and a suspected one. Until services can establish which compound a patient actually took, the psychiatric literature on this group will continue to rest on suspicion.

## Glossary

- **Synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonist (SCRA)** — A laboratory-made compound that fully switches on the brain's CB1 cannabinoid receptor, unlike cannabis, which only partly activates it.
- **Synthetic cathinone** — A stimulant built on the chemical frame of the khat plant, acting on the same brain transporters as amphetamine.
- **Toxidrome** — The recognisable cluster of signs that a class of poison produces, used to narrow down the substance before laboratory results arrive.
- **Immunoassay** — The quick antibody-based urine drug screen used in most emergency departments; it finds only the substances it was designed to look for.
- **Rhabdomyolysis** — Breakdown of muscle tissue that releases proteins into the blood and can cause kidney failure.
- **Schneiderian first-rank symptoms** — A set of psychotic experiences, such as hearing voices comment on one's actions, historically treated as characteristic of schizophrenia.
