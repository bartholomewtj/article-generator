**Subject:** neuroleptic malignant syndrome in the emergency department

**Question.** What is known about neuroleptic malignant syndrome in the emergency department?

**Answer.** Neuroleptic malignant syndrome in the emergency department is supported mainly by retrospective cohorts and case reports rather than comparative trials. The syndrome may present with the familiar combination of hyperthermia, rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability, and muscle injury, but atypical cases can lack fever, marked rigidity, or substantial creatine kinase elevation. Diagnostic uncertainty is substantial because malignant catatonia, serotonin toxicity, infection, heat illness, and metabolic disease can overlap clinically. Complications described in emergency presentations include rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, respiratory failure, metabolic derangement, recurrent episodes, and death. A systematic review of case reports found improvement after lorazepam in many reported cases, but treatment effects remain highly uncertain because the evidence is uncontrolled and heterogeneous.

## What remains open

- No prospective emergency-department study has validated diagnostic criteria for atypical neuroleptic malignant syndrome against important mimics such as malignant catatonia, serotonin toxicity, infection, or heat illness.
- Comparative trials have not established whether lorazepam, bromocriptine, dantrolene, amantadine, or supportive care produces better outcomes in emergency presentations.
- The extent to which creatine kinase concentration, blood pressure, renal indices, or other laboratory markers independently predict mortality remains uncertain beyond small retrospective cohorts.[1,2]

## Glossary

- **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome** — A rare, potentially fatal reaction associated mainly with dopamine-blocking or other dopamine-modulating medicines.
- **Atypical neuroleptic malignant syndrome** — A presentation in which one or more usual features, such as fever, severe rigidity, or marked creatine kinase elevation, are absent or mild.
- **Rhabdomyolysis** — Breakdown of skeletal muscle that releases intracellular substances into the circulation and can cause kidney injury.
- **Malignant catatonia** — A severe catatonic state that can include stupor, rigidity, fever, and autonomic instability and may resemble neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
