**Question.** What is known about catatonia in the emergency department?

**Answer.** Catatonia in the emergency department is clinically important but remains supported mainly by observational and case-based evidence. A national administrative study indicates that coded presentations are uncommon yet frequently lead to admission, while a neurology emergency case series found that catatonia was repeatedly assigned alternative diagnoses. Reports across infectious, metabolic, structural, psychiatric, and substance-associated presentations show that the syndrome can accompany serious medical disease and may evolve rapidly into autonomic instability. A small mood-disorder cohort reported rapid symptom remission after benzodiazepine treatment, but this was not a controlled comparison. The available material does not establish a comparative emergency-department treatment pathway. Recognition, etiologic investigation, and acute treatment remain insufficiently studied in undifferentiated emergency populations.

## What the evidence shows

*Every figure in this review was located in the material read for the cited sources — their abstracts, and the open-access full text where one was retrieved.*

- Luccarelli et al. conducted a weighted national administrative study of emergency-department visits from 2019 to 2021; 51,671 visits included a catatonia diagnosis, representing 0.013% of presentations, with hospital admission in 82.6% and documented physical restraint in 5.6%.[1]
- Anand et al. reviewed a case series of 12 patients aged 22–55 years discharged from a neurology service; emergency neurology residents identified none as catatonia, commonly assigning diagnoses such as extrapyramidal syndrome, meningitis, or non-convulsive status epilepticus.[2]
- Huang et al. retrospectively evaluated 12 patients with mood-disorder-associated catatonia, including 10 with major depressive disorder and 2 with bipolar disorder, type one; eight remitted after one lorazepam dose, two after a second dose, and the remaining two improved with diazepam.[3]
- Ferraz de Abreu et al. reported a 46-year-old woman with new-onset seizures, fever, mutism, waxy flexibility, catalepsy, and stupor in the emergency department; cerebrospinal-fluid testing identified herpes simplex type 1 encephalitis, and she improved after intravenous acyclovir.[4]
- Azizi et al. described a 58-year-old man with diabetes and human immunodeficiency virus who presented with rigidity and mutism; bedside glucose was 29 milligrams per deciliter, and dextrose administration restored his baseline status.[5]
- Antoniadis et al. reported a 47-year-old woman with human immunodeficiency virus, bipolar disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder who presented to a psychiatric emergency department with new catatonia; imaging revealed a large frontal cerebral abscess, followed by gradual neurological and mental-status improvement after surgical treatment.[6]
- Garfinkel et al. described a 52-year-old man with schizophrenia and catatonia who boarded in a psychiatric emergency department for more than 24 hours before developing cardiac arrest from extensive bilateral pulmonary emboli, with subsequent ischemic encephalopathy.[7]

## What remains open

- The national administrative study measured coded diagnoses rather than undiagnosed catatonia, so the extent of missed recognition in emergency departments remains uncertain.
- No comparative study in undifferentiated emergency-department patients has established which diagnostic assessment strategy best distinguishes catatonia from delirium, intoxication, seizures, or neurological disease.
- The relative effectiveness and safety of benzodiazepines, electroconvulsive therapy, and treatment of underlying medical causes remain uncertain because the available treatment evidence is largely uncontrolled.
- The prevalence and presentation of catatonia across pediatric, community, rural, and nonpsychiatric emergency settings remain insufficiently characterized.

## Glossary

- **Catatonia** — A neuropsychiatric syndrome involving abnormal changes in movement, speech, behavior, or responsiveness.
- **Malignant catatonia** — A severe catatonia presentation associated with fever, autonomic instability, and potentially life-threatening medical complications.
- **Autonomic instability** — Dangerous fluctuations in functions such as heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, or breathing that are regulated automatically by the body.
