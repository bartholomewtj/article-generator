**Subject:** catatonia in the emergency department

**Question.** How does catatonia present, how frequently is it diagnosed, and how is it evaluated and managed in the emergency department?

**Answer.** Catatonia appears to be an underrecognized neuropsychiatric syndrome in emergency settings, recorded in a small fraction of presentations but associated with high inpatient admission rates exceeding eighty percent. Presentations span all age groups, exhibiting what appears to be a bimodal distribution and stemming from primary psychiatric disorders as well as underlying medical and neurological conditions. Emergency evaluation centers on distinguishing catatonia from delirium and toxic-metabolic encephalopathies, often incorporating diagnostic lorazepam challenges. Although initial benzodiazepine administration frequently provides rapid temporary relief of core motor and speech abnormalities, catatonic episodes may carry notable risks of physical restraint use, autonomic deterioration, and rapid symptom recrudescence.

- In a national emergency database study of presentations between 2019 and 2021, catatonia was coded in 0.013% of all presentations, demonstrated a bimodal age distribution peaking at 20-29 and 55-64 years, had an 82.6% inpatient admission rate, and involved physical restraint use in 5.6% of visits.[1]
- The same nationwide analysis identified primary psychiatric conditions in 55.5% of catatonia encounters and noted that Black patients accounted for an apparently disproportionate share of diagnoses at 27.6%.[1]
- A clinical review of emergency literature described catatonia as an often overlooked etiology of altered consciousness and stupor that arises across diverse organic and psychiatric conditions, potentially leading to diagnostic delays.[2]
- In a psychiatric emergency study evaluating lorazepam challenge dosing, administration of a 2 mg dose achieved statistically significant resolution across multiple individual signs, including stupor, mutism, staring, posturing, rigidity, negativism, echolalia, and mitgehen.[3]
- A report of emergency department resuscitation presentations suggested that rapid motor and speech improvements following an initial lorazepam challenge unmasked underlying psychosis, but symptoms appeared to revert within hours without sustained intervention.[4]
- A retrospective pediatric hospital cohort evaluated in the emergency department and inpatient wards observed a significant increase from 3.9 to 9.7 catatonia cases per 1,000 psychiatry consults between 2018-2020 and 2021-2023, with autoimmune encephalitis identified in 70% of strictly medical etiologies.[5]

## What remains open

- The true prevalence of emergency department catatonia remains uncertain due to widespread underdiagnosis and variable use of standardized bedside screening instruments.
- Optimal standardized dosing schedules and observation timelines for emergency lorazepam challenge tests have not been evaluated in large prospective randomized trials.
- Comparative outcomes and trajectories between emergency-identified catatonia driven by primary psychiatric illness versus acute general medical etiologies remain insufficiently studied.

## Glossary

- **Catatonia** — A complex neuropsychiatric syndrome characterized by motor, behavioral, and vocal abnormalities such as stupor, mutism, posturing, and negativism.
- **Lorazepam challenge test** — The diagnostic and therapeutic administration of a test dose of lorazepam to observe rapid temporary relief of catatonic signs.
- **Stupor** — A state of profound immobility, mutism, and reduced responsiveness while maintaining preserved wakefulness.
- **Mitgehen** — An automatic motor obedience where a patient moves a limb in response to slight touch despite instructions to resist.
