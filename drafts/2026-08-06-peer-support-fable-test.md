# Peer support workers in acute mental health care show promise for reducing readmission but the direct evidence base remains thin

*Generated 6 August 2026 · 12 sources cited, 2012–2026 · Abstract-derived synthesis · Not peer reviewed*

**Subject:** Peer support workers in acute mental health care: what the evidence shows

**Abstract.** Peer support workers are people with lived experience of mental illness who are trained and employed to support others through their own recovery. Their deployment has expanded rapidly across community mental health services internationally, yet acute settings — inpatient wards, crisis teams and emergency departments — have adopted the model far more slowly, in part because coercive and risk-focused practices sit uneasily alongside recovery-oriented ways of working. Whether peer support improves outcomes for people in mental health crisis is therefore a live policy question with a surprisingly narrow evidence base. Here we review the direct evidence on peer support in acute care and place it in the context of the broader peer support literature. The strongest direct evidence comes from a large randomised trial of peer-facilitated self-management after crisis team discharge, which reported a significant reduction in readmission to acute care; a scoping review of inpatient studies documents feasibility, diverse peer roles and recurrent implementation barriers, while a trial of peer-enhanced crisis response in emergency departments is under way. Meta-analytic evidence from mixed service settings suggests reliable gains in self-reported recovery and empowerment but not in clinical symptoms or service use, a tension the acute-care literature has yet to resolve. Rigorous trials in inpatient settings remain a priority.

**Keywords:** peer support workers; acute psychiatric inpatient care; crisis resolution teams; psychiatric readmission; lived experience workforce; recovery-oriented practice; mental health services

## Key points

- In a randomised controlled trial of 441 people discharged from six English crisis resolution teams, up to ten sessions with a peer support worker facilitating a self-management workbook reduced one-year readmission to acute care from 38% to 29% (odds ratio 0.66, 95% CI 0.43–0.99).[8]
- A 2025 scoping review found only eleven studies of peer support in acute psychiatric inpatient settings published between 1995 and 2024, reporting feasibility and recovery-related benefits but recurrent problems of unclear role boundaries, staff resistance, emotional strain and poor team integration.[4]
- A meta-analysis of nineteen trials of one-to-one peer support (3,329 participants) across mental health services found modest gains in self-reported recovery and empowerment but no effect on clinical symptoms or service use, in tension with the readmission reduction seen in the crisis-team trial.[3,8]
- A small randomised study of 74 repeatedly hospitalised patients found that assignment of a peer 'recovery mentor' significantly reduced both admissions and hospital days over nine months compared with usual care.[1]
- A stepped-wedge cluster-randomised trial across five US emergency departments is testing whether a peer support enhanced crisis response team reduces physical restraint and sedation use; the protocol acknowledges that evidence for peer support in the emergency setting is currently limited.[10]
- A systematic review of 41 randomised trials found that peer worker training ranged from a few hours to 192 hours with inconsistent reporting, underscoring how variably the intervention is implemented across the evidence base.[6]

## Introduction

Peer support workers — staff employed on the basis of their own lived experience of mental illness and recovery — have become one of the fastest-growing elements of the mental health workforce. The practice has a long lineage, traceable to the hiring of recovered patients at the Bicêtre Hospital in the 1790s, and estimates a decade ago already placed the peer workforce at more than ten thousand in the United States alone.[1] Contemporary reviews describe an internationally growing trend to embed peer support within mental health and addiction services, driven by workforce policy as much as by evidence.[2,3]

Acute care is the setting where this expansion has lagged furthest behind. Inpatient wards, crisis resolution teams and emergency departments concentrate coercion, risk management and rapid turnover, all of which constrain recovery-oriented practice and complicate the peer role.[4] Recent international recommendations for comprehensive schizophrenia care nonetheless list peer support among the components of real-world service provision, including inpatient care.[5] Here we review what the direct evidence shows about peer support workers in acute mental health care, drawing on adjacent literatures — community peer support, substance use services and care transitions — where the acute-setting evidence is absent, and labelling such extrapolation explicitly.

## Feasibility and implementation in inpatient settings

The most direct synthesis of inpatient evidence is a 2025 scoping review that identified eleven studies, published between 1995 and 2024, of peer support in acute psychiatric inpatient settings.[4] Implementation varied from informal ward presence to structured programmes differing in frequency, continuity and delivery context, and peer roles encompassed emotional, practical, advocacy and relational support. Reported benefits included increased activity participation, emotional reassurance, social engagement and recovery-related gains for patients and for the peer workers themselves. The review's authors are careful to note that the evidence maps feasibility rather than establishing effectiveness, and that structural and cultural features of acute wards continue to constrain adoption.[4]

The same review catalogues recurrent implementation problems: unclear role boundaries, staff resistance, emotional strain on peer workers, and structural limitations of space, resources and team integration.[4] These findings echo the wider peer support literature. A systematic review of forty-one randomised trials of peer support for serious mental health challenges found that collaboration, providing support and communication were the most frequently deployed competencies, but that reporting of training — which ranged from a few hours to 192 hours — and of supervision was inconsistent across studies.[6] Davidson and colleagues, drawing on two decades of programme experience, similarly emphasise clear role definitions, training in the deliberate use of one's own recovery story, employing at least two peer workers per unit, and supervision attentive to the distinct demands of the role.[1] A narrative synthesis of thirteen papers on peer support in personality disorder services reached a compatible conclusion: implementation appears valuable when supervision, training and boundary maintenance are provided intentionally, but the evidence base is small.[7]

## Effects on readmission and acute care use

The strongest single piece of direct evidence concerns the transition out of acute care. A randomised controlled superiority trial in England recruited 441 participants from the caseloads of six crisis resolution teams and offered the intervention group up to ten sessions with a peer support worker, who supported completion of a personal recovery workbook including recovery goals and crisis plans; controls received the workbook by post.[8] Within one year, 29% of intervention participants were readmitted to acute care compared with 38% of controls, an odds ratio of 0.66 (95% CI 0.43–0.99; p=0.0438). The authors themselves hedge appropriately: admission rates were lower than anticipated, confidence intervals were wide, and the complexity of the intervention makes it difficult to attribute the effect to the peer element specifically rather than to the self-management content it delivered.[8]

Smaller studies point in the same direction. Davidson and colleagues describe a randomised study of 74 recently and repeatedly hospitalised patients assigned to usual care or usual care plus a peer 'recovery mentor', which reported significantly fewer hospitalisations and fewer hospital days over nine months in the mentored group.[1] Earlier comparative studies summarised in the same review had likewise detected reduced hospitalisation and better engagement of difficult-to-reach clients when services were peer-delivered.[1]

This apparent consistency is challenged by meta-analytic evidence from broader service settings. A systematic review of nineteen trials of one-to-one peer support, providing data from 3,329 participants, found no impact on clinical symptoms or on service use, with modest positive effects confined to self-reported recovery and empowerment.[3] The discrepancy may reflect population and intervention differences: the trials pooled in the meta-analysis spanned paraclinical roles, structured behavioural interventions and flexible recovery support across heterogeneous populations, whereas the crisis-team trial targeted a defined post-crisis window with a structured self-management protocol.[3,8] Which of these features carries the effect remains unresolved.

## Psychosocial outcomes and proposed mechanisms

Where peer support does show reliable effects, they are predominantly psychosocial. The meta-analysis of one-to-one peer support reported modest improvements in self-reported recovery and empowerment, with exploratory analyses suggesting possible gains in social network support.[3] A three-arm randomised study of 290 adults with serious mental illness, described by Davidson and colleagues, found that adding peer-facilitated person-centred care planning increased participants' sense of control, and that a peer-run community connector programme increased hope, engagement in illness management, social support and community belonging — though it also, unexpectedly, increased distress attributed to residual symptoms as participants pursued fuller lives.[1]

The mechanisms proposed for these effects are specific to the peer role: instillation of hope through positive self-disclosure, role modelling of self-care and experiential 'street smarts', and a relationship characterised by trust, mutual understanding and empathy grounded in shared experience.[1] These mechanisms map plausibly onto acute settings, where the inpatient scoping review reports emotional reassurance and relational support as core peer functions.[4] Delivery formats are also diversifying: a single-arm pilot in 21 community-dwelling adults with serious mental illness and medical comorbidity found that a 12-week digital peer-supported self-management intervention was feasible and acceptable and was associated with statistically significant improvements in self-efficacy for chronic disease management and personal empowerment, although the design permits no causal inference.[9]

## Crisis care beyond the ward

Acute care extends beyond inpatient units, and the emergency department is an emerging frontier. A protocol has been published for a stepped-wedge cluster-randomised trial, conducted over three years across five emergency departments in the northeastern United States, of a peer support enhanced behavioural crisis response team; the primary outcome is the rate of physical restraint and/or sedation use.[10] The trial rests on the observation that patients experience restraint as coercive and dehumanising and that peer involvement has shown positive outcomes in behavioural crises elsewhere, but it explicitly notes that evidence for the approach in the emergency setting is limited — results are not yet available.[10]

Transitions from acute care are a further point of application. A systematic review of 45 papers on interventions to improve discharge from acute adult mental health wards identified peer support as one recurring component among discharge interventions targeting readmission, wellbeing, homelessness and suicide, but found outcome reporting too heterogeneous — 69 distinct outcomes across studies — to permit quantitative synthesis.[11] Evidence from substance use services offers adjacent, extrapolated support: a systematic review of peer recovery support services reported tentatively positive findings on substance use, treatment retention and treatment satisfaction, alongside many null results and methodological limitations including the inability to isolate the peer contribution from co-delivered supports.[12] That confounding problem applies with equal force to complex acute-care interventions such as the crisis-team trial.[8,12]

## Conclusions and outlook

The direct evidence on peer support workers in acute mental health care is sparse but coherent. One adequately powered randomised trial indicates that peer-facilitated self-management after crisis-team discharge reduces readmission over one year;[8] a scoping review of eleven inpatient studies establishes feasibility, delineates roles and identifies consistent implementation barriers without confirming effectiveness;[4] and a cluster-randomised trial in emergency departments is in progress.[10] Everything else — psychosocial benefits, mechanisms, competencies — is carried over from community and mixed-setting literatures and should be read as extrapolation.[1–3,6]

The central open question is whether the readmission benefit observed after crisis-team discharge generalises to peer support delivered on inpatient wards themselves, where the meta-analytic record shows no effect on service use in mixed settings.[3,8] Resolving it will require randomised trials embedded in acute wards, with outcomes selected to match the plausible mechanisms of peer support — hope, empowerment and engagement — as well as service use, and with the peer element separable from co-delivered content.[3,12] Equally, the implementation literature is unambiguous that training, supervision, role clarity and staff-culture work are preconditions rather than refinements.[1,4,6,7] Trials that neglect them are likely to test poorly implemented peer support rather than peer support itself.

## Glossary

- **peer support worker** — A person with personal experience of mental illness and recovery who is trained and employed to support others with similar conditions.
- **crisis resolution team** — A community mental health team that provides intensive short-term treatment at home as an alternative to hospital admission during a mental health crisis.
- **scoping review** — A structured review that maps the extent and nature of evidence on a topic without formally judging the effectiveness of interventions.
- **stepped-wedge cluster-randomised trial** — A trial design in which whole sites are switched from control to intervention in a randomised sequence until all sites deliver the intervention.
- **recovery-oriented care** — An approach to mental health care that prioritises a person's own goals, hope and self-determination over symptom control alone.
- **odds ratio** — A measure comparing the odds of an outcome between two groups, where values below one indicate a lower likelihood in the intervention group.
