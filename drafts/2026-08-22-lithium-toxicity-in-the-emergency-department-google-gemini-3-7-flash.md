**Subject:** lithium toxicity in the emergency department

**Question.** What are the clinical presentations, precipitating factors, diagnostic challenges, and acute management strategies for lithium toxicity in emergency department settings?

**Answer.** Presentations of lithium toxicity in the emergency department can range from mild gastrointestinal disturbance and tremors to encephalopathy, seizures, and potentially life-threatening cardiac conduction abnormalities. Intoxication is commonly categorized into acute, acute-on-chronic, and chronic forms, where chronic exposures may present with disproportionate neurotoxicity that seems to correlate imperfectly with circulating serum concentrations. Precipitating factors often involve reduced renal clearance triggered by volume depletion or newly initiated interacting medications. Diagnostic evaluation generally relies on urgent serum lithium measurements, renal chemistry panels, and electrocardiography, alongside vigilance for potential preanalytical artifacts such as lithium heparin tube collection. Management typically focuses on fluid resuscitation and supportive care, while extracorporeal removal via hemodialysis is usually reserved for severe poisoning, marked neurological impairment, or compromised renal function.

- In a nested case-control study of 3,115 ambulatory lithium users, the observed prevalence of acute care visits for toxicity was 2.2%, with newly initiated interacting drugs representing the strongest apparent risk factor (odds ratio 30.30, 95% confidence interval 2.32 to 394.95).[2]
- Neuroimaging in a 30-year-old woman presenting with acute-on-chronic toxicity and a serum level of 3.54 mmol/L revealed cytotoxic lesions of the corpus callosum that appeared to fully resolve at 5-month follow-up after hemodialysis.[3]
- Emergency treatment of a 48-year-old patient presenting in deep coma with a serum concentration of 4.5 mmol/L demonstrated that two sequential sessions of expanded hemodialysis with medium cut-off membranes could achieve sustained clearance, reducing levels to 0.1 mmol/L within 72 hours.[4]
- Application of a simplified six-electrode electroencephalogram in the emergency department facilitated the rapid detection of nonconvulsive status epilepticus in a 65-year-old woman with acute renal dysfunction and a serum level of 3.88 mEq/L.[5]
- Cardiovascular evaluation of an 80-year-old woman presenting with impaired consciousness and a level of 2.55 mEq/L revealed sinus node dysfunction that necessitated temporary pacing, with rhythm disturbances appearing to resolve completely following drug elimination.[6]
- Diagnostic evaluation in a 58-year-old woman highlighted potential preanalytical error when collection in a lithium heparin tube produced an apparent concentration of 2.05 mmol/L, whereas retesting demonstrated a true concentration below 0.10 mmol/L.[7]
- Administration of telmisartan in a 62-year-old woman on stable lithium therapy for 15 years precipitated symptomatic neurotoxicity with a level of 1.55 mmol/L, suggesting that angiotensin receptor blockers may significantly impair renal lithium excretion.[8]

## What remains open

- Controlled comparative trials remain lacking regarding whether novel medium cut-off dialyzers or continuous renal replacement techniques offer advantages over conventional intermittent hemodialysis in acute poisoning.
- Evidence evaluating gastrointestinal decontamination methods, such as whole-bowel irrigation for large or sustained-release lithium overdoses, appears largely restricted to observational case series without randomized trial validation.
- The optimal clinical thresholds for initiating extracorporeal removal in moderate chronic intoxication without coma remain uncertain, likely reflecting the frequent discordance between circulating serum concentrations and central nervous system toxicity.

## Glossary

- **cytotoxic lesions of the corpus callosum** — Transient magnetic resonance imaging abnormalities showing cellular edema in the splenium, associated with toxic or metabolic encephalopathies.
- **nonconvulsive status epilepticus** — A prolonged seizure state without convulsive motor activity, presenting primarily as altered mental status and diagnosed by electroencephalography.
- **expanded hemodialysis** — A dialysis modality utilizing medium cut-off membranes designed to improve clearance of medium-to-large molecular solutes.
