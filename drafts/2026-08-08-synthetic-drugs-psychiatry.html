SubjectNovel psychoactive substances and acute psychiatric harm: synthetic cannabinoids and cathinones

Link copied

AbstractNovel psychoactive substances are synthetic compounds engineered to reproduce the effects of controlled drugs while falling outside the legislation that governs them. More than 950 have been catalogued by European monitoring bodies since the early 2000s, and two classes account for most of the psychiatric burden attributed to the group: synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists and synthetic cathinones. Both arrive in emergency and psychiatric services as agitation, paranoia and frank psychosis, and neither is reliably detected by the antibody-based urine screens that most services run, so the exposure is often invisible at the point of assessment. Here we review the evidence on acute and persisting psychiatric harm from these two classes, drawing on systematic reviews, hospital case series and toxicologically confirmed intoxication cohorts. The evidence indicates that receptor agonists carry a more severe and longer psychotic course than cannabis, that cathinones produce a stimulant picture close to methamphetamine but with greater physical complication, and that the two classes diverge in how long their symptoms and their analytes persist. Most of the literature remains retrospective, and prospective psychiatric follow-up after analytically confirmed exposure is scarce. Analytical capacity and surveillance, rather than unfamiliar pharmacology, appear to be the limiting factors.

Keywords novel psychoactive substances; synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists; synthetic cathinones; substance-induced psychosis; toxicovigilance; emergency psychiatry

Introduction

Illicit drug markets have carried a parallel synthetic catalogue since the mid-2000s. These compounds are built from the molecular scaffolds of controlled drugs, sold through internet vendors under labels such as research chemicals or legal highs, and replaced as soon as a jurisdiction schedules them.1,2 More than 900 have now been identified.14 A clinician assessing an intoxicated patient is therefore working without the pharmacological reference points that accumulate around an established drug.

Two classes dominate the psychiatric burden. Synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists bind the CB1 receptor with considerably greater potency than tetrahydrocannabinol, while synthetic cathinones act on monoamine transporters in the manner of amphetamine-type stimulants.3 Of the 163 substances reported to European monitoring between 2010 and 2012, 39.3% were synthetic cannabinoids and 16.6% were synthetic cathinones.4 Their share of clinical presentations exceeds their share of the catalogue.

Here we review what the published work establishes about the mental health consequences of using these two drug groups, and what it leaves open. The scope is clinical rather than chemical: presentation, course, detection and outcome, rather than the question of which analogue emerges next. Sources range from systematic reviews of a whole class to single-centre series of analytically confirmed intoxications, and their evidential weight differs accordingly. Where the two lines of work disagree, the discrepancy is likely to reflect that difference in design rather than a genuine change in the drugs.

Acute presentations of synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists

The syndrome attributed to these agonists is consistent across reviews of the class. A public-health review of synthetic and plant-derived cannabinoids reported anxiety, agitation, psychosis, suicidal ideation and cognitive impairment alongside respiratory difficulty, hypertension, tachycardia and acute renal failure, locating the divergence from natural cannabis in receptor potency.5 That account is plausible but untested against a natural comparison group. A systematic review of 109 intoxication cases in which psychiatric symptoms were documented found hallucinations, aggressiveness and bizarre behaviour to be the commonest acute features, with synthetic cannabinoids the class most often implicated.6

Whether exposure initiates a psychotic illness or unmasks a latent vulnerability remains unresolved. A systematic review that screened 4477 records and retained 95 identified these agonists as the substances most frequently linked to acute psychosis, and noted Schneiderian first-rank symptoms in some presentations, a phenomenological overlap with schizophrenia that the authors read as a possible risk of transition.7 Many episodes appeared transient, yet a proportion evolved into persistent psychotic states, an observation compatible with either causal account.

Description from the ward itself is thinner. Four patients admitted to acute psychiatric services with psychosis and confirmed agonist misuse were assessed with the PANSS and monitored with the National Early Warning Signs tool; the authors concluded that management turned on identifying the index substance, increasing nursing observation and tracking vital signs.8 A series of four cannot establish a syndrome, though it remains one of the few reports to document what inpatient services actually did. Findings from so small a group might not generalise beyond the unit that produced them.

Method. A PRISMA 2020 systematic review searching six databases for the period January 2005 to August 2025. Primary inclusion required longitudinal studies with at least six months of psychiatric follow-up, with severe acute complications and fatalities admitted separately so that the toxicity spectrum was not narrowed to survivors.

Results. Fifty-eight studies were retained, 28 (48.3%) of them offering at least six months of follow-up. In one comparative cohort, hospitalisation for synthetic cannabinoid-related psychosis ran to 43.45 days against 22.91 for cannabis-related psychosis; rhabdomyolysis reached 63% in a cathinone outbreak series, and NBOMe exposures carried fatality rates near 15% and intensive care admission near 40%.

Limitations. The synthesis was narrative rather than meta-analytic, and only 22 of the 58 studies (37.9%) were rated high quality. The headline comparisons rest on single cohorts and case series rather than pooled estimates, so the magnitudes should be read as indicative rather than as class-wide effect sizes.

Synthetic cathinones and the stimulant picture

Cathinones present as stimulant intoxication, and the closest available comparison is with methamphetamine. A psychiatric emergency department in Taiwan recruited 24 patients with cathinone intoxication between April 2019 and May 2020 and matched them by sex and age to 48 with methamphetamine intoxication, confirming exposure by mass spectrometry rather than antibody screening.9 Severity of psychotic symptoms was similar between the groups, as were the risks of violence and self-harm. The finding suggests that the acute mental state alone is unlikely to distinguish the two exposures at the bedside.

The difference lay elsewhere. Physical complications were substantially more common in the cathinone group, at an odds ratio of 8.55 with a confidence interval of 2.15 to 34.03, while family histories of substance use and criminal records were less prevalent among those patients.9 A review of the cathinone and cannabinoid designer classes reported that chronic cathinone use can induce acute psychosis, hypomania, paranoid ideation and delusions resembling amphetamine-type stimulant psychosis.10 Severe intoxication has separately been linked to serotonin syndrome and to potentially fatal rhabdomyolysis.4 Whether the physical excess reflects the compounds themselves or the route and pattern of their use remains unclear from a study of this size.

Time course separates the two classes in a way that matters at triage. A Japanese series of six patients aged 22 to 42 was analysed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry. Cathinone-related psychiatric and neurological symptoms proved long-lasting, with the compounds detectable in serum for 15 to 48 hours. Cannabinoid symptoms resolved within several hours, and serum concentrations stayed at or below 5 ng/mL for one to three hours.11 One patient with high concentrations of both drugs died, heart failure being judged the most plausible cause. Six patients are too few to fix these windows precisely, but the correspondence between analyte persistence and symptom duration would be expected on pharmacokinetic grounds.

Receptor pharmacology and the potency problem

The clinical picture tracks the receptor pharmacology closely enough that the class largely predicts the toxidrome. Stimulant compounds either inhibit dopamine and noradrenaline transport or induce release of those monoamines; entactogens predominantly enhance serotonin release; hallucinogens act as direct agonists at 5-HT2A receptors; and synthetic cannabinoids agonise CB1.1 The ratio of dopamine to serotonin transporter inhibition measured in vitro has been proposed as a predictor of both the sought effect and the toxicity. Serotonergic compounds tend to be associated with serotonin syndrome, hyperthermia, seizures and hyponatraemia, and dopaminergic compounds with prolonged stimulation, insomnia, agitation and psychosis.1

Potency, rather than an unfamiliar mechanism, appears to drive the excess harm. A review of designer drugs concluded that their mechanisms broadly resemble those of traditional drugs of abuse, and placed the cannabinoid difference in full agonism at CB1 where tetrahydrocannabinol is only a partial agonist.2 A pharmacological review of the cannabinoid class counted 207 substances detected by European monitoring between 2008 and October 2020 and attributed the association with acute and chronic intoxication to that full agonism.12 An update covering stimulant and hallucinogenic compounds grouped their effects into raised central dopamine, activation of 5-HT receptor subtypes and antagonism at NMDA receptors, and argued that clinicians require regular briefing on what is in circulation.13

Review of individual compounds — mitragynine, the Spice cannabinoids, mephedrone and methylone, ketamine and gamma-hydroxybutyrate among them — shows the same pattern of a known target reached by an unfamiliar molecule.3 Sex and gender differences in response remain largely uncharacterised for this group, although men present more often to emergency departments with serious or fatal intoxication while women seem equally likely to develop a substance use disorder and possibly more susceptible to craving and relapse.14

Detection and the limits of routine screening

Routine drug screening does not see these compounds, and that absence shapes every clinical encounter with them. Both classes often escape the panels used in screening tests,4 and the systematic review of psychiatric effects treated missing toxicological confirmation as a source of diagnostic error rather than a technical inconvenience.6 A diagnosis of substance-induced psychosis otherwise rests on an exposure the laboratory has never documented. The true frequency of these exposures in psychiatric presentations is therefore uncertain, and published counts are probably underestimates.

A retrospective multicentre series from Reunion Island quantifies that gap. Of 176 patients admitted to four public hospitals with suspected exposure between August 2022 and March 2024, 152 (86%) underwent biological analysis and 14 (9.2%) were confirmed positive by high-resolution mass spectrometry.15 The confirmed compounds spanned synthetic cathinones, synthetic cannabinoids, a novel opioid and an aminoindane, and clinical severity ranged from agitation and sedation through to cardiovascular collapse, rhabdomyolysis and respiratory failure.

That cohort also describes who presents. Intentional use accounted for 77.3% of exposures and fell overwhelmingly to men, while unintentional exposure made up the remainder and was significantly more frequent among women.15 Previous psychoactive substance use was reported by 92.9% of the confirmed group against 42.0% of the rest, and alcohol co-exposure was less common in confirmed cases than in conventional intoxications. On these figures a polydrug history is a better prompt to test than any single presenting sign.

Course, mortality and surveillance

Evidence on what follows the acute episode is the weakest part of this literature, and the most recent systematic review is explicit about why. Searching six databases for January 2005 to August 2025 under PRISMA 2020, it retained 58 studies, of which 28 (48.3%) offered at least six months of psychiatric follow-up and 22 (37.9%) reported acute toxicity or fatalities.16 The same proportion, 22 studies, was rated high quality. Estimates of persisting illness drawn from a base of this composition are consistent with a wide range of true values.

Where follow-up existed, the figures point one way. Hospitalisation for synthetic cannabinoid-related psychosis ran markedly longer than for cannabis-related psychosis in one comparative cohort, at 43.45 against 22.91 days, and a case series recorded psychotic episodes persisting beyond five months in approximately 30% of patients.16 Rhabdomyolysis reached 63% in a cathinone outbreak series, while NBOMe exposures carried a fatality rate near 15% and intensive care admission near 40%.

Mortality surveillance adds a policy dimension that clinical series cannot. Fifteen years of post-mortem toxicology reported to a national programme covering over 85% of coroners in England, Wales and Northern Ireland showed detections of novel stimulants peaking in 2015, novel opioids in 2017, and synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists in 2018 and again in 2021.17 The generic Chinese ban on cannabinoid structural families in May 2021 was followed by their near-total disappearance from these deaths in 2022, whereas the domestic legislation introduced earlier had no comparable effect.17 Availability, on that reading, is set in part by producer countries rather than by consumer-country prohibition alone. Post-mortem detection is a lagging and selective measure, so the timing of these peaks may reflect testing practice as well as supply.

Key points

  • A matched comparison in a psychiatric emergency department found equivalent psychotic severity between 24 cathinone and 48 methamphetamine intoxications, but an odds ratio of 8.55 for physical complications in the cathinone group.9
  • Hospitalisation for synthetic cannabinoid-related psychosis lasted 43.45 days against 22.91 for cannabis-related psychosis in one comparative cohort, and roughly 30% of one case series remained psychotic beyond five months.16
  • A toxicologically confirmed series analysed 152 of 176 suspected intoxications and confirmed only 14, indicating how much suspected exposure never reaches biological confirmation.15
  • Serum detection windows diverge sharply between the classes: 15 to 48 hours for cathinones against one to three hours for cannabinoids in a six-patient series, matching the reported duration of symptoms.11
  • Detections of synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists in United Kingdom deaths fell almost to zero in 2022 after generic controls were introduced in China in 2021, while earlier domestic legislation showed no equivalent effect.17
  • Fewer than half the studies in the most recent systematic review offered six months of psychiatric follow-up, and only 22 of 58 were rated high quality.16

Conclusions and outlook

Two findings rest on more than a single study. Synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists produce psychosis that is more severe and longer-running than the cannabis-related form, supported by systematic review, comparative hospitalisation data and repeated case description.6,7,16 Cathinones produce a psychotic and behavioural presentation close to methamphetamine while carrying greater physical risk, supported by a matched emergency-department comparison and by class reviews.9,10

What stays open is the direction of causation in psychosis, the true incidence of persisting illness, and whether outcome differs between analogues within a class. Nearly every source here is retrospective or a narrative synthesis; the prospective element, where there is one, is the toxicological confirmation rather than the psychiatric follow-up. A cohort that confirmed exposure analytically at presentation and then tracked psychiatric outcome for a year or more would settle more than any further case series.

The practical implication is unglamorous. Analytical capacity and toxicovigilance networks, not new therapeutics, are what these sources repeatedly identify as missing, given that treatment for the intoxications themselves is supportive.1 Until services can establish which compound a patient actually took, the psychiatric literature on this group will continue to rest on suspicion.

Glossary

Synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonist (SCRA)
A laboratory-made compound that fully switches on the brain's CB1 cannabinoid receptor, unlike cannabis, which only partly activates it.
Synthetic cathinone
A stimulant built on the chemical frame of the khat plant, acting on the same brain transporters as amphetamine.
Toxidrome
The recognisable cluster of signs that a class of poison produces, used to narrow down the substance before laboratory results arrive.
Rhabdomyolysis
Breakdown of muscle tissue that releases proteins into the blood and can cause kidney failure.
Schneiderian first-rank symptoms
A set of psychotic experiences, such as hearing voices comment on one's actions, historically treated as characteristic of schizophrenia.
Toxicovigilance
The organised monitoring of poisonings across a health system so that new hazards are spotted early.
