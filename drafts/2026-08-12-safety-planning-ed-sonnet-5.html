AbstractSuicidal ideation and suicide attempts are common reasons for presentation to hospital emergency departments (EDs), and an ED visit represents a high-risk period during which follow-up care is often not established. The Safety Planning Intervention (SPI), a brief, collaboratively developed list of warning signs, coping strategies, support contacts, and steps to limit access to lethal means, has become a widely recommended brief intervention for suicidal patients in acute care settings. Its evidence base, however, has developed unevenly across populations and health systems, and questions remain about which components drive benefit and how consistently the intervention is delivered as designed. Here we review evidence for SPI delivered in ED and related acute-care settings, drawing on cohort comparisons, stepped-wedge trials, systematic reviews, and implementation studies from the United States, Canada, France, and the United Kingdom. Evidence in adults, particularly within Veterans Health Administration systems, associates SPI combined with structured follow-up with reductions in suicidal behavior and increased outpatient engagement. Evidence in adolescents is markedly weaker: a recent meta-analysis found no significant association between standalone safety planning and suicide-related outcomes in youth. Several large randomized trials are underway across multiple countries, and their results should help clarify effect sizes, optimal delivery format, and whether adolescent-specific adaptation is required.

Introduction

Suicidal ideation and suicide attempts are common reasons for presentation to hospital emergency departments (EDs), and many people who later die by suicide have contact with acute health care in the months beforehand.1 Suicide accounts for more than 800,000 deaths worldwide each year, most in low- and middle-income countries.1 Suicide risk in children and adolescents has drawn increasing clinical attention: a recent overview reports a marked rise in youth suicide rates and describes a brief four-item screening tool that identifies most at-risk adolescents who endorse it.2 Because psychiatric admission is not indicated for most ED patients with suicidal ideation, and outpatient referral is often unavailable or delayed, the ED encounter is frequently the only structured opportunity to intervene before discharge.3

The Safety Planning Intervention (SPI) is a brief, collaboratively developed tool that helps a patient identify personal warning signs, coping strategies, sources of support, and steps to restrict access to lethal means during a future crisis.4 It requires no specialist mental health training and can typically be completed within a single ED encounter.5 SPI has since been adapted for telephone follow-up, nurse-led delivery, and combination with structured psychotherapy, and has been written into national suicide-prevention policy in more than one health system.6,7 Here we review evidence for SPI and related brief interventions in ED and acute-care settings, considering adult, veteran, and adolescent populations separately.

Reduced suicidal behavior in adult cohorts

The strongest direct evidence in adults comes from a cohort comparison across nine Veterans Health Administration EDs, five delivering SPI with structured telephone follow-up (SPI+) and four providing usual care.4 Among 1640 patients, most of them men in their late 40s, suicidal behavior over six months was documented in 3.03% of the SPI+ group versus 5.29% of usual care.4 This corresponds to roughly 45% fewer suicidal behaviors and approximately half the odds of an event (odds ratio 0.56; 95% CI, 0.33-0.95).4 SPI+ patients also had more than double the odds of attending at least one outpatient mental health visit.4 Because sites rather than patients were assigned to condition, site-level confounding cannot be excluded.

A stronger design came from the ED-SAFE 2 trial, a stepped-wedge cluster randomized study across eight US EDs that combined universal suicide risk screening with collaborative safety planning within a quality-improvement package.8 Across three sequential 12-month phases and 2761 patient encounters, a composite outcome of suicide death or suicide-related acute care visit fell from 21% at baseline to 15.3% during maintenance, an adjusted reduction of approximately 43%.8 Because the intervention bundled screening, coaching, and safety planning together, this trial cannot isolate safety planning's independent contribution.

A systematic review of brief psychological interventions identified four controlled studies across Switzerland, the US, and several low- and middle-income countries, together enrolling 3412 participants who received safety planning combined with early engagement and follow-up sustained for at least 12 months.1 Two of the four studies reported fewer suicide attempts and one reported fewer deaths by suicide, but two studies that measured suicidal ideation directly found no effect on that outcome.1 A methodological review of 20 SPI studies in adults, mostly veterans or ED patients, concluded that results generally support reductions in ideation, behavior, and hospitalization alongside increased outpatient engagement, while noting substantial heterogeneity in outcome measures.9

Method. Cohort comparison study across nine Veterans Health Administration emergency departments (five delivering the intervention, four usual care), enrolling 1640 patients 18 years or older presenting for suicide-related concerns, with suicidal behavior and outpatient service use tracked from medical records over six months following discharge.

Results. Patients who received the Safety Planning Intervention with structured telephone follow-up (SPI+) engaged in suicidal behavior less often than those receiving usual care (3.03% vs 5.29%), corresponding to about 45% fewer suicidal behaviors and roughly half the odds of a suicidal event (odds ratio 0.56; 95% CI, 0.33-0.95); SPI+ patients also had more than double the odds of attending at least one outpatient mental health visit.

Limitations. The cohort comparison design assigned intervention status by site rather than by individual patient randomization, so site-level differences in practice or population could confound the association, and the sample was drawn from a largely male veteran population, limiting generalizability.

Implementation within veteran health systems

Much of the adult evidence derives from the Veterans Health Administration (VHA), which has embedded safety planning into national policy. A qualitative study of 50 staff informants across five VA EDs found that almost all clinicians perceived SPI with structured follow-up as helpful for connecting patients to care.5 A slight majority believed it increased safety, and clinicians' comfort discharging at-risk patients increased after implementation.5

Restricting access to lethal means is a core SPI component. An electronic health record study of VHA ED and urgent care visits between January 2021 and October 2022 found that 15.2% of 17,194 patients discharged home after suicide risk evaluation were documented as having firearm access.10 Among the 2624 with documented access, 80.6% received a safety intervention within 24 hours, most often review or creation of a safety plan (88.6%), with lethal means counseling (56.8%) and cable lock distribution (13.2%) less frequent.10 Firearm access itself may be under-recorded, which would understate true need.

Two large VA cohort evaluations examined outcomes linked to programs that mandate or encourage safety planning. An archival analysis of 14,743 patients evaluated for suicide risk between October 2018 and May 2023, with a discharged-home subgroup of 7024, found that documented safety planning was associated with a 26% reduction in odds of all-cause mortality within one year.7 The association did not extend to 30-day mental health attendance, and suicide-specific mortality was not separately examined.7 In REACH VET, a program targeting the top 0.1% of predicted suicide risk, a triple-differences cohort study of more than 173,000 veterans found program entry associated with more completed outpatient appointments, more new safety plan documentation, and fewer mental health admissions, ED visits, and suicide attempts.11 Subcohort analyses detected no difference in suicide or all-cause mortality.11 Both studies are observational and cannot isolate safety planning's specific contribution.

This concentration of VA research partly reflects acuity. A cross-sectional study using VHA's national data found that veterans screened in ED or urgent care were substantially more likely than those screened in ambulatory care to be classified at high acute risk (34.1% versus 8.5%).12 This finding is tangential to SPI effectiveness itself but reinforces why EDs are prioritized for brief interventions.12

Adolescents and self-harm populations

Evidence in adolescents is considerably thinner and less favorable. A systematic review and meta-analysis identified ten studies of SPI as a standalone treatment in 1002 adolescents (mean age 15.0 years; 76.0% female), of which five studies (n = 619) contributed pooled estimates.13,14 The meta-analysis found no significant association between safety planning and suicidal ideation (Hedges g = 0.11; 95% CI, 0.01-0.21), suicide-related behavior (Hedges g = -0.09; 95% CI, -0.20 to 0.02), suicide attempts (risk ratio 1.03; 95% CI, 0.12-8.88), or re-presentation to care (risk ratio 0.99; 95% CI, 0.29-3.35).13,14 Risk of bias was rated moderate to high in most included studies.13,14 This null result contrasts with the more consistent behavioral findings in adult cohorts, and the authors concluded that available data do not support safety planning as a standalone treatment in youth.

A broader umbrella review of 23 systematic reviews, spanning adult and mixed adult-adolescent liaison psychiatry populations, reached a more favorable conclusion for brief interventions generally.15 Person-centred brief contact approaches incorporating safety planning and follow-up were most consistently associated with reduced suicide attempt rates, while longer structured therapies showed benefit for repeat self-harm with longer follow-up.15 Heterogeneity and mixed-age samples mean these findings cannot be read as adolescent-specific support.

Several adolescent-specific trials are underway but unreported. SAFE STEPS is comparing developmentally tailored ED safety planning alone against safety planning combined with 12 months of follow-up contact in 1600 adolescents and young adults aged 13 to 24, with suicide attempts and outpatient linkage as primary outcomes.16 In the UK, a trial of the SASH intervention, combining therapeutic assessment, an enhanced safety plan, and solution-focused brief therapy, recruited 154 young people across nine EDs; the available report illustrates delivery through case studies rather than trial outcomes.17 A narrative review of youth ED safety planning outlines a practical delivery framework but represents expert synthesis rather than new empirical evidence.18

Delivery quality and ongoing trials

Qualitative work suggests that how SPI is delivered may matter as much as whether it is delivered. A study interviewing seven patients and six clinicians at a single Canadian psychiatric ED identified six recommendations: personalizing plan content, offering varied formats, avoiding periods of high emotional intensity, involving a broad range of professionals, working within limited time, and offering alternatives when a plan is unsuitable.3 Within the same paper, a cited national survey of more than 2000 US hospitals found that only two of six standard safety-plan elements were typically discussed with at-risk patients more than half the time.3

Two ongoing multicentre randomized trials aim to test delivery models directly. PROTECT, a stepped-wedge cluster trial across 20 French EDs, is comparing nurse-led SPI against an existing recontact program in a target sample of 2387 people who have attempted suicide, with suicidal behavior at six months as the primary outcome.6 ASSURED, a UK two-arm trial in patients with self-harm, is testing up to five rapid post-discharge contacts, including an enhanced safety planning session, against usual care in 620 participants across 14 sites.19 Its primary outcome is ED re-attendance and liaison psychiatry referral at 18 months; recruitment concluded in December 2024.19

Key points

  • A cohort comparison across nine VHA emergency departments found SPI with structured telephone follow-up associated with roughly 45% fewer suicidal behaviors and more than double the odds of outpatient engagement compared with usual care.4
  • The ED-SAFE 2 stepped-wedge trial across eight US EDs reported a drop in the composite suicide-related outcome from 21% at baseline to 15.3% after department-wide adoption of a quality-improvement package built around collaborative safety planning.8
  • A meta-analysis of ten studies in 1002 adolescents found no significant association between standalone safety planning and suicidal ideation, behavior, attempts, or health care re-presentation, contrasting with more favorable adult findings.13,14
  • An electronic health record study of VHA emergency and urgent care visits found that among patients with documented firearm access, most received a safety intervention, but firearm access itself was documented in only a minority of at-risk patients.10
  • A qualitative study of patients and clinicians at a Canadian ED found that only two of six standard safety-plan elements were typically discussed with at-risk patients more than half the time, pointing to inconsistent fidelity in routine practice.3
  • An umbrella review of 23 systematic reviews across liaison psychiatry settings found brief contact interventions incorporating safety planning were the intervention type most consistently linked to reduced suicide attempt rates.15

Conclusions

The evidence for SPI in EDs is strongest, and most consistent, in adults, particularly within the Veterans Health Administration. A cohort comparison and a stepped-wedge trial both reported reductions in suicide-related behavior after implementation of safety planning combined with follow-up or a broader quality-improvement package,4,8 and observational VA program evaluations reported parallel associations with reduced mortality risk and reduced suicide attempts.7,11 None of these designs isolates safety planning from the follow-up, screening, or care-coordination elements delivered alongside it, so its independent contribution remains inferred rather than demonstrated.

In adolescents, the available meta-analytic evidence does not support safety planning as an effective standalone treatment, despite its widespread adoption in pediatric EDs.13,14 Qualitative and survey data further suggest inconsistent fidelity to the full SPI model in routine practice.3 Resolving these open questions will require convergent results from the large randomized trials now underway across France, the UK, and the US.6,16,17,19 These trials are designed to isolate safety planning's contribution, test developmentally tailored versions for youth, and use standardized outcomes over follow-up periods long enough to capture reattempt risk. Until they report, SPI should be regarded as a broadly acceptable component of ED suicide-prevention practice whose independent effect size, and applicability to adolescents, remain to be established.

Glossary

Safety Planning Intervention (SPI)
A brief, collaboratively developed plan that lists an at-risk person's warning signs, coping strategies, support contacts, and steps to limit access to lethal means during a crisis.
Stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial
A trial design in which all sites eventually receive the intervention, but the order and timing of rollout is randomized, allowing before-and-after comparison within each site.
Lethal means counseling
Discussion with an at-risk person and their family about reducing access to methods commonly used in suicide, such as firearms or medications.
Odds ratio
A measure of association comparing the odds of an outcome in one group to the odds in another; a value below 1 indicates lower odds in the first group.
Hedges g
A standardized measure of effect size for continuous outcomes, similar to Cohen's d but adjusted for small sample sizes.
Umbrella review
A synthesis of multiple systematic reviews on a topic, used to summarize evidence across many prior reviews rather than individual studies.
