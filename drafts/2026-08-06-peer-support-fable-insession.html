Peer support in acute mental health care is promising but implementation-dependent

SubjectPeer support workers in acute mental health care: what the evidence shows

AbstractPeople with personal experience of mental illness are increasingly employed to support others through formal peer worker roles. Mental health systems internationally have embraced these roles in community services, yet acute settings — inpatient wards and crisis teams — remain the environments where recovery-oriented practice is hardest to deliver and where the case for peer support is least tested. Admission and the weeks that follow discharge carry concentrated risks, including readmission, disengagement and distress, so an intervention grounded in shared experience is an attractive candidate at this point in the care pathway. Here we review what controlled trials, systematic reviews and implementation studies indicate about peer support around acute care. The evidence suggests a benefit for readmission in individual trials, modest improvements in self-rated recovery and empowerment in pooled analyses, and no consistent effect on clinical symptoms. Outcomes appear to depend in part on implementation: preparation of ward teams, supervision and role clarity distinguish services where peer support engages patients from services where it struggles. Rigorous trials conducted on wards themselves remain scarce, and adequately powered studies now underway may clarify whether early promise translates into routine practice.

Keywords peer support workers; acute mental health care; psychiatric readmission; lived experience workforce; crisis resolution team; implementation fidelity

Introduction

Peer support workers are employed in mental health services on the basis of their own lived experience of mental illness and recovery.1 The idea has a longer history than its recent growth implies: recovered patients were hired into a Paris asylum as early as 1793, and later estimates placed the peer workforce above ten thousand in the United States alone.2 Policy guidance across many countries now recommends paid peer roles, and consensus recommendations for comprehensive psychosis care include peer support among their components.3

Acute services are a distinctive test of that policy commitment. Wards and crisis teams operate under sustained pressure, and structural and cultural constraints appear to limit recovery-oriented practice in these environments.4 Transitions out of hospital carry concentrated risk, which makes the discharge period a natural target for support grounded in shared experience.5

Here we review the evidence bearing on peer support in and around acute mental health settings, drawing on randomised trials, systematic and umbrella reviews, and implementation studies. Findings from community services, substance use care and personality disorder services are considered where they inform acute practice, and are labelled as extrapolation.

Box 1 | Key study: Peer-supported self-management for people discharged from a mental health crisis team: a randomised controlled trial

Johnson et al. (2018) · The Lancet6 · doi:10.1016/s0140-6736(18)31470-3

It is the only adequately powered randomised trial to test a peer-delivered intervention against readmission to acute mental health care.

Method. Participants recruited from six crisis resolution teams in England were randomly assigned to up to ten sessions with a peer support worker facilitating a personal recovery workbook, or to receiving the workbook by post.

Results. Readmission to acute care within one year occurred in 29% of the intervention group versus 38% of controls, a statistically significant difference. Serious adverse events were fewer in the intervention arm.

Effects on readmission and acute service use

The strongest direct evidence comes from a randomised controlled superiority trial recruiting from six crisis resolution teams in England.6 Participants discharged from crisis care were offered a series of sessions with a peer worker, who supported them to complete a personal recovery workbook; controls received the workbook by post. By one year, 64 of 218 participants in the intervention group (29%) had returned to acute care, compared with 83 of 216 controls (38%), a statistically significant difference that the investigators interpreted cautiously because the confidence interval was wide.6

Smaller programmes point the same way. A review spanning two decades of peer support research describes a controlled study in which 74 participants with repeated admissions were assigned peer recovery mentors or usual care, and those with mentors recorded fewer admissions and fewer hospital days over nine months.2 Replication at scale is underway: a pragmatic stepped-wedge cluster-randomised trial in Alberta aims to recruit 10,000 participants leaving acute psychiatric units and day hospitals, with readmission within 30 days as its primary outcome.7

Pooled analyses are less encouraging. A meta-analysis of nineteen trials of one-to-one peer support, together enrolling 3329 participants, found no impact on service use or on clinical symptoms.8 The discrepancy with the trial evidence above remains unresolved; it may reflect pooling of heterogeneous roles and settings, since the included interventions ranged from paraclinical case-management support to flexible recovery work, and few targeted acute care.8

Psychosocial and recovery outcomes

Self-reported outcomes appear more responsive than clinical endpoints. The same meta-analysis reported a modest positive impact on self-reported recovery and empowerment, with heterogeneity analyses hinting that peer support might strengthen social network support.8 An umbrella review of 35 reviews covering 426 primary studies reached a compatible conclusion: peer support may improve depression symptoms, self-efficacy and recovery, although nearly all of the included reviews were rated as low quality.9

Trial evidence adds texture to the pooled estimates. In a three-arm randomised study of 290 adults with serious mental illness, peer-facilitated person-centred planning increased participants' sense of control over their care, and a peer-run community connector programme increased hope, engagement and community belonging.2 That study also recorded reduced psychotic symptoms alongside greater distress about them, which the investigators attributed in part to participants attempting more in their lives.2

Not every synthesis reads the field favourably. A broad literature review notes that several studies report neutral effects from integrating peer services and question whether resources are well spent, even as others emphasise gains in hope, engagement and quality of life.1

Implementation in acute settings

Whether peer support works in acute care seems inseparable from how it is introduced. A scoping review located eleven studies of peer support on acute psychiatric wards published between 1995 and 2024, describing informal and structured formats and roles that span emotional, practical, advocacy and relational support.4 Reported difficulties included unclear boundaries, resistance from ward staff, emotional strain on peer workers, and shortages of space and resources.4

Implementation quality appears to track engagement. In a comparative case study nested within a randomised trial, interviews with 20 peer workers and their supervisors showed that sites combining prepared clinical teams, resourced supervision and positive ward relationships achieved high engagement and high fidelity to peer support principles.10 Sites with constrained supervision, rural geography or employment through external organisations struggled on both counts, and engagement in the parent trial was associated with readmission outcome.10

Syntheses of the wider peer workforce point to the same levers. Across the umbrella review's implementation evidence, adequate training and supervision, a recovery-oriented workplace, strong leadership and a trusting culture facilitated success, while lack of time, funding and recognised certification recurred as barriers.9

Adjacent populations and settings

Evidence from neighbouring fields is informative but constitutes extrapolation rather than direct support. In services for people with personality disorder, a systematic review identified thirteen papers describing varied peer roles, with early signals of symptom improvement and greater satisfaction where deployment was intentional, supervised and boundaried.11

Substance use services offer a cautionary parallel. A systematic review of peer recovery support services found tentative positives — reduced substance use, better treatment retention and stronger relationships with providers — set against many null findings and designs that could not isolate the contribution of peer support from other recovery activities.12

Discharge-focused syntheses place peer support among many candidate interventions rather than above them. A narrative synthesis of 45 papers on improving transitions from acute wards grouped peer support alongside named models such as the Critical Time Intervention, and found the 69 outcomes reported across studies too heterogeneous for quantitative comparison.5

Conclusions and outlook

Three conclusions appear defensible. Peer-facilitated self-management after a crisis reduced readmission in one adequately powered trial.6 Pooled and observational findings are consistent with modest benefit to self-rated recovery, empowerment and mood, while clinical symptom change is the least supported claim.8 Direct evaluations conducted on acute wards themselves remain scarce and largely descriptive.4

The open questions are specifiable. The Alberta stepped-wedge trial will indicate whether peer support combined with supportive text messaging shifts 30-day readmission at scale.7 Ward-based trials that measure fidelity, of the kind the implementation literature points towards, would show whether team preparation and supervision are preconditions of effect rather than correlates of engagement.10 Until such studies report, the defensible position is that peer support in acute settings is promising, sensitive to implementation, and not established as a routine component of care.

Glossary

Crisis resolution team
A service that treats people at home during a mental health crisis as an alternative to hospital admission.
Peer support worker
A person employed in mental health care on the basis of their own lived experience of mental illness and recovery.
Stepped-wedge trial
A trial design in which all sites eventually receive the intervention, crossing over from control in a randomised sequence.
Fidelity
The degree to which an intervention is delivered as its model intends.
Umbrella review
A systematic review whose units of analysis are other systematic reviews.
