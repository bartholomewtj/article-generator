**Subject:** lithium toxicity in the emergency department

**Question.** What is known about lithium toxicity in the emergency department?

**Answer.** The available direct evidence appears to be dominated by retrospective observational research and case reports rather than randomized emergency-department studies. Presentations are heterogeneous, with neurological features particularly prominent in chronic or acute-on-chronic toxicity, while renal impairment, dehydration, age, and medication interactions commonly accompany accumulation. Serum lithium concentration does not consistently appear to reflect neurological severity. Renal replacement therapy has been used selectively, but a large multicenter observational study reported no significant outcome difference according to adherence to consensus criteria. Case reports have described recovery after supportive care or hemodialysis, but have also reported persistent neurological injury and reversible cardiac conduction abnormalities. The evidence is consistent with uncertainty regarding the relationship between clinical assessment and lithium measurement, treatment thresholds, outcome prediction, and prevention of long-term neurotoxicity.

## What the evidence shows

*Every figure in this review was located in the material read for the cited sources — their abstracts, and the open-access full text where one was retrieved.*

- The multicenter SILITOX retrospective observational study analysed 548 lithium-poisoning episodes from emergency departments and toxicology units; 119 received renal replacement therapy, and adherence to EXTRIP criteria was not associated with differences in persistent neurotoxicity, critical-care admission, or in-hospital mortality.[1]
- Heath and colleagues’ nested case-control study of 3,115 lithium users identified 70 toxicity cases; newly initiated potentially interacting medication was reported to be strongly associated with acute-care use for toxicity, while older age and greater chronic disease burden showed smaller associations.[2]
- Kobylianskii and colleagues’ case report of a woman with chronic lithium toxicity reported altered mental status, weakness, involuntary movements, and renal complications despite only a minimally elevated serum concentration; neurological recovery was reported after fluid treatment and hemodialysis.[3]
- Kronk and colleagues’ emergency-department case report of a man reported tremor, hallucinations, ataxia, and cognitive decline with an elevated lithium concentration; neurological symptoms had resolved by the fourth hospital day after lithium withdrawal and fluid treatment.[4]
- Heidari and colleagues’ case report of a woman reported chronic lithium toxicity with loss of consciousness, severe sinus bradycardia, and an elevated serum concentration; cardiac and neurological abnormalities were reported to have resolved after lithium discontinuation.[5]
- Miura and colleagues’ case report of a woman with acute-on-chronic toxicity reported impaired consciousness, myoclonus, renal impairment, and a high lithium concentration; corpus-callosum lesions and neurological function subsequently improved after hemodialysis and supportive care.[6]
- Pérez Del Valle and colleagues’ case report of a man with coma and a lithium concentration of 4.5 mmol/L reported substantial concentration reduction and full neurological recovery after two expanded-hemodialysis sessions, but the report could not establish comparative effectiveness.[7]

## What remains open

- No randomized or prospective comparative emergency-department study has established which patients might benefit most from hemodialysis or other renal replacement strategies.
- The relationship between serum lithium concentration, tissue burden, neurological severity, and persistent neurotoxicity has appeared inconsistent, particularly in chronic and acute-on-chronic poisoning.
- Predictors of the syndrome of irreversible lithium-effectuated neurotoxicity and whether early extracorporeal treatment changes that risk remain uncertain.

## Glossary

- **Acute-on-chronic toxicity** — Poisoning that occurs when a person already taking lithium ingests more than the maintenance amount.
- **EXTRIP** — An international expert group that develops consensus recommendations for extracorporeal treatment of poisoning.
- **Renal replacement therapy** — Treatment such as hemodialysis that temporarily performs functions normally carried out by the kidneys.
- **SILENT** — Persistent neurological impairment attributed to lithium toxicity after the acute poisoning has resolved.
